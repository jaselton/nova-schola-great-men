## The Essential Question

> What can students learn from Frederick's life that no textbook can teach?

Frederick the Great teaches that power can be treated as work rather than luxury. He inherited a disciplined Prussian state, turned himself into its tireless chief administrator, and insisted that a ruler must labor for the state rather than merely enjoy its honors. He read, wrote, reformed courts, promoted religious toleration, patronized music and philosophy, and made Prussia one of Europe's great powers.

But Frederick also warns students that discipline without humility can become cold ambition. His seizure of Silesia in 1740 helped launch years of European war. His victories were brilliant, but they cost thousands of lives and hardened Prussia around military power. He could speak like a philosopher and act like a conqueror. He is therefore a useful figure precisely because he is not simple: a ruler of real industry and intelligence, and also a warning about greatness separated from charity and restraint.

## Life Overview

### Early Formation (1712-1740)

**Family Background**  
Frederick was born in Berlin, the eldest surviving son of Frederick William I of Prussia and Sophia Dorothea of Hanover. His father was a severe ruler who prized economy, obedience, religion, and military discipline. Frederick loved French literature, music, philosophy, and refined conversation. Father and son collided painfully.

**Humiliation and the Katte Crisis**  
As a young prince, Frederick tried to escape Prussia with the help of Hans Hermann von Katte. The plan failed in 1730. Frederick was imprisoned at Küstrin, and Katte was executed in his presence. This event marked Frederick deeply. It taught him the terrible seriousness of royal power and may have helped form the hard self-command that later defined him.

**Rheinsberg and Intellectual Formation**  
After partial reconciliation with his father, Frederick spent happier years at Rheinsberg, where he read, wrote, played music, corresponded with Enlightenment thinkers, and composed political reflections. His *Anti-Machiavel*, published around the time of his accession and edited with Voltaire's involvement, attacked cynical political deception. The tension of his life appears here early: he praised moral kingship, then soon acted with ruthless strategic opportunism.

### Rising Action (1740-1756)

**Accession and Silesia**  
Frederick became king in 1740. Within months he invaded Silesia, a wealthy Habsburg province. His dynastic claims were weak, but the opportunity was strong: Emperor Charles VI had died, Maria Theresa was vulnerable, and Prussia had a well-prepared army and treasury. The First and Second Silesian Wars ended with Prussia holding most of Silesia.

This made Prussia a major European power. It also revealed Frederick's central contradiction. He thought intensely about just rule, but he was willing to begin war for advantage.

**The Working King**  
Frederick did not use kingship merely for ceremony. He read reports, corrected officials, inspected provinces, reviewed troops, and wrote instructions. He strengthened the bureaucracy, promoted legal reforms, supported economic projects, and cultivated an image of the king as a servant of the state. He was not a constitutional ruler in the modern sense. He was an absolute monarch. But he expected the monarch to work.

### Peak Achievement and Crisis (1756-1763)

**The Seven Years' War**  
The Seven Years' War placed Prussia in danger of destruction. Austria wanted Silesia back; France, Russia, Saxony, and Sweden aligned against Prussia, while Britain became Frederick's chief ally. Frederick opened the war by invading Saxony. He won major victories, including Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757, but also suffered severe losses. For years Prussia survived by discipline, maneuver, British subsidies, enemy disunity, and fortune.

The war nearly broke him. By 1762, the death of Empress Elizabeth of Russia and the accession of Peter III dramatically changed Russia's policy toward Prussia. The Treaty of Hubertusburg in 1763 left Frederick in possession of Silesia. Prussia survived and emerged recognized as a great power.

**What the War Revealed**  
Frederick's courage and endurance were real. He shared danger with his army, recovered after defeat, and maintained state discipline under immense pressure. Yet the war also showed the cost of power politics. His strategic brilliance cannot erase the human cost of his ambitions.

### Later Rule (1763-1786)

After the war, Frederick worked to rebuild. He promoted agriculture, settlement, drainage projects, industry, and administrative efficiency. He supported judicial reform and professional standards in the bureaucracy. German History in Documents and Images notes his abolition of judicial torture and the opening of royal appeals courts to villagers in conflict with noble lordships.

Frederick also cultivated culture. He built Sanssouci at Potsdam, played the flute, wrote in French, and corresponded with intellectuals including Voltaire. But his later years became more solitary and severe. His wit could become contempt. His high standards could become misanthropy. He died in 1786 after forty-six years of rule.

## Defining Moments

### The Failed Escape and Katte's Execution (1730)

**Context:** Frederick hated his father's harsh militarism and tried to flee.

**Decision:** He risked escape instead of submitting quietly.

**Action:** The plan failed; Katte was executed, and Frederick was imprisoned.

**Consequence:** Frederick learned fear, self-command, and the unforgiving nature of state power.

**Lesson:** Pain can form discipline, but it can also harden the soul.

### The Invasion of Silesia (1740)

**Context:** Maria Theresa inherited Habsburg lands in a vulnerable moment.

**Decision:** Frederick chose opportunity over restraint.

**Action:** Prussia invaded Silesia in December 1740.

**Consequence:** Prussia gained a rich province and rose toward great-power status, but Europe was drawn into wider war.

**Lesson:** Strategic brilliance must be judged morally, not only by success.

### Rossbach and Leuthen (1757)

**Context:** Prussia faced powerful enemies in the Seven Years' War.

**Decision:** Frederick relied on speed, discipline, and maneuver rather than surrender.

**Action:** He won striking victories at Rossbach and Leuthen.

**Consequence:** Prussia survived when many expected collapse.

**Lesson:** Prepared discipline can preserve a people in crisis; but the crisis itself may have been partly chosen.

### The First Servant of the State

**Context:** Eighteenth-century monarchs often treated states as dynastic possessions.

**Decision:** Frederick described the sovereign as the first servant of the state.

**Action:** He worked intensely, supervised administration, and treated rule as labor.

**Consequence:** He helped define the image of enlightened absolutism.

**Lesson:** Authority should mean responsibility before privilege.

## Failures and Recovery

### Aggressive Ambition

**What Went Wrong:**  
Frederick attacked Silesia at the beginning of his reign and later opened the Seven Years' War by invading Saxony. His choices served Prussian power but helped produce immense suffering.

**Why It Happened:**  
He saw politics through strategic opportunity, state interest, and the harsh logic of eighteenth-century power competition. He also inherited a state built around military readiness.

**How He Recovered:**  
After 1763 he focused heavily on reconstruction, agriculture, administration, and law. But reconstruction does not undo the moral cost of aggressive war.

**What They Learned:**  
Students should not confuse victory with justification. Success answers the question, “Did it work?” It does not answer, “Was it right?”

### Philosophy Without Full Moral Consistency

**What Went Wrong:**  
Frederick wrote against Machiavellian cynicism, yet his own policy could be opportunistic and deceptive.

**Why It Happened:**  
He separated private philosophical ideals from the pressures of kingship and international competition.

**How He Recovered:**  
He never fully resolved the contradiction. He remained both a writer of enlightened principles and a practitioner of hard power.

**What They Learned:**  
Ideas must be tested by actions. A student should ask not only what a leader says, but what his choices reveal.

### Hardness of Soul

**What Went Wrong:**  
Frederick's discipline could become contempt. His later judgments of human nature often turned harsh.

**Why It Happened:**  
A severe childhood, years of war, and long experience of bureaucratic resistance likely intensified his coldness.

**How He Recovered:**  
He maintained duty and work, but not always warmth. His recovery was administrative more than personal.

**What They Learned:**  
Duty is necessary, but duty without charity can become iron.

## Teaching Wisdom

### Core Lessons

1. **Power Is Work**  
   Frederick's best lesson is that leadership is not decoration. A ruler, captain, teacher, or parent must labor for the good of those entrusted to him.

2. **Discipline Can Save a State**  
   Prussia's survival depended on preparation, training, economy, and repeated effort under pressure.

3. **Culture and Command Can Belong Together**  
   Frederick was a soldier-king who also loved music, literature, architecture, and philosophy. Students should resist the false choice between strength and cultivation.

4. **Success Is Not the Same as Justice**  
   The conquest of Silesia made Frederick famous. It also reveals why ambition needs moral judgment.

5. **The State Is Not God**  
   Frederick's language of service is admirable, but students should see the danger of making state power the highest good.

### Discussion Questions

**For Ages 6-10:**
- What does it mean for a king to work for his people?
- Why should strong people be careful with their power?
- Can someone be brave and still make wrong choices?

**For Ages 11-14:**
- Why did Frederick's father and Frederick clash?
- Was taking Silesia a wise action, a wrong action, or both?
- How can discipline help a family, school, or country survive difficulty?

**For Ages 15-18:**
- Is enlightened absolutism a contradiction?
- How should we judge Frederick's statement that the sovereign is the first servant of the state?
- Can a ruler serve the state while harming neighboring peoples?

## Primary Sources and Source Notes

### Key Quotations

> “The sovereign is the first servant of the State.”  
> — Frederick II, *Political Testament* (1752), in German History in Documents and Images translation

This line captures Frederick's theory of kingship as duty and labor. It should be admired, but also tested against his wars.

> “As well may the sun hide its spots, as great princes their vices and their genuine character, from the eyes of so many curious observers.”  
> — Frederick II, *Anti-Machiavel*, chapter XVIII, English translation excerpt in German History in Documents and Images

This is a useful teaching line because Frederick understood that rulers cannot hide character forever.

### Historically Grounded Source Notes

- Encyclopaedia Britannica gives Frederick's birth in Berlin on January 24, 1712, death at Potsdam on August 17, 1786, reign from 1740 to 1786, enlargement of Prussia through wars against Austria and other powers, admiration for French art and language, and construction of Sanssouci.
- Britannica's Frederick biography describes his severe upbringing under Frederick William I, conflict over artistic and intellectual tastes, the failed 1730 escape, imprisonment at Küstrin, and Katte's execution in Frederick's presence.
- Britannica's account of the War of the Austrian Succession states that Frederick invaded Silesia on December 16, 1740, and that the Treaty of Dresden in 1745 confirmed Prussian possession of Silesia.
- Britannica's Seven Years' War entry describes the alignment of Austria, France, Saxony, Sweden, and Russia against Prussia, Hanover, and Great Britain, and explains that Austria sought to regain Silesia.
- German History in Documents and Images summarizes Frederick's forty-six-year reign, administrative and judicial reforms, cultural projects, abolition of judicial torture, professional preparation in judiciary and bureaucracy, and his later high-handedness and misanthropy.
- German History in Documents and Images provides excerpts from Frederick's *Anti-Machiavel* and the *Political Testament*, including the “first servant of the State” formulation.
- The Voltaire Foundation describes *Anti-Machiavel* as a collaboration between Frederick and Voltaire: Frederick's rebuttal of *The Prince*, edited and expanded by Voltaire.
- Teach Sanssouci as part of Frederick's cultivated self-presentation, not as evidence that his reign was gentle. Culture and hardness coexisted.

## For the AI Tutor

### When to Reference This Figure

- **Leadership:** duty, administrative seriousness, work before privilege.
- **History:** War of the Austrian Succession, Seven Years' War, rise of Prussia, Enlightenment monarchy.
- **Ethics:** success vs. justice, power and restraint, ambition and responsibility.
- **Civics:** state service, bureaucracy, law reform, religious toleration under monarchy.

### Tutorial Moves

- Ask students to distinguish efficiency from virtue: “Did it work?” and “Was it right?”
- Use Frederick when a student admires power too simply.
- Contrast Frederick with Washington when discussing restraint.
- Use his “first servant” line to ask what leadership costs the leader.

### Cautions

- Do not make Frederick a simple hero of progress. His wars matter morally.
- Do not make him a simple villain. His discipline, reforms, and service ethic were real.
- Do not equate enlightened absolutism with modern liberty.
- Do not treat Prussia's rise as inevitable or bloodless.

## Final Synthesis

Frederick the Great belongs in the Great Men sequence because he forces students to think carefully about power. He was brilliant, disciplined, cultivated, and tireless. He understood that a ruler should work for the state, not merely consume its honors. He helped make Prussia a great power and gave Europe a model of enlightened absolutism.

Yet his greatness is morally dangerous. He could reform courts and begin wars. He could write against deception and practice hard opportunism. He could love flute music and send armies into brutal campaigns. He reminds students that talent, discipline, and success do not settle the deepest questions.

The lesson is not “be like Frederick” without qualification. The lesson is: work with Frederick's seriousness, prepare with his discipline, and accept responsibility with his sense of duty. But judge ambition before it becomes conquest. Let strength be governed by justice, and let service be larger than the state.
