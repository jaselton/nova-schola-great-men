# Thomas Jefferson: One-Week Lesson Plan
## The Pen of Liberty and the Life of Contradiction

---

## Overview

This one-week study teaches Thomas Jefferson through the theme of principle and practice: words, liberty, conscience, education, and the moral contradiction of slavery.

**Formation Aim:** Students should understand that true words are powerful, but they must become true actions.

---

## Before You Begin

### Materials Needed

- Student lesson for the appropriate age band
- Notebook or binder page for Jefferson
- Short excerpt from the Declaration of Independence
- Short excerpt from the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom
- Map of the United States before and after the Louisiana Purchase
- Optional: image of Monticello and/or the University of Virginia Rotunda

### Choose Your Age Band

- **Ages 8-10:** Focus on words, learning, equality, and truthfulness.
- **Ages 11-14:** Add liberty of conscience, Louisiana Purchase, and contradiction.
- **Ages 15-18:** Add natural rights, republican education, constitutional tension, and slavery as moral failure.

---

## Monday: The Story

### Morning: Read Together (20-30 min)

Read the profile overview and the appropriate student lesson.

Ask:
- What words is Jefferson most remembered for?
- Why did he care about learning?
- What hard truth must we tell about him?

### Afternoon: Start a Jefferson Notebook (15-20 min)

Create a page with four boxes:

1. **Writer** — Declaration of Independence
2. **Defender of Conscience** — religious freedom
3. **President and Builder** — Louisiana Purchase, University of Virginia
4. **The Hard Truth** — slavery at Monticello

Younger students may draw pictures. Older students should write three bullet points per box.

---

## Tuesday: The Declaration

### Morning: Memory Work (5 min)

Copy or recite:

> True words should become true actions.

Older students may add:

> “All men are created equal.”

### Afternoon: Close Reading (25-40 min)

Read the opening principles of the Declaration of Independence.

Discuss:
- What truth does Jefferson state?
- Why would the colonies need to explain themselves to the world?
- How can one sentence become a standard for later generations?

Written response:

**Ages 8-10:** Copy “all men are created equal” and explain it in your own words.  
**Ages 11-14:** Write five sentences on why the Declaration mattered.  
**Ages 15-18:** Explain how the Declaration judges both British rule and American slavery.

---

## Wednesday: Conscience and Education

### Morning: Religious Liberty (20-30 min)

Read:

> “Almighty God hath created the mind free...”

Discuss:
- Can belief be forced?
- What is the difference between persuasion and coercion?
- Why does conscience matter in a free society?

### Afternoon: Design a School for Liberty (30-45 min)

Jefferson believed citizens needed education for self-government.

Ask the student to design a one-page school plan for a republic. Include:
- Three subjects every citizen should study
- One virtue every citizen should practice
- One danger education should help prevent

Older students should compare their plan with Jefferson's University of Virginia vision.

---

## Thursday: Presidency and Expansion

### Morning: Map the Louisiana Purchase (25-35 min)

Use a map to compare the United States before and after 1803.

Discuss:
- Why was the purchase such a major opportunity?
- Why might Jefferson have had constitutional doubts?
- What new problems came with expansion?

### Afternoon: Mixed Statesmanship Chart (25-35 min)

Make two columns:

**Achievements** | **Dangers / Consequences**

Add examples:
- Doubled U.S. territory | Conflict over Native lands
- Opened western expansion | Future spread of slavery
- Showed practical judgment | Stretched constitutional principles

Older students should write a paragraph: “Was the Louisiana Purchase prudent?”

---

## Friday: Moral Honesty and Synthesis

### Morning: The Hard Truth (20-30 min)

Teach plainly and calmly:

Jefferson enslaved people at Monticello. The man who wrote about equality did not grant equality to many people under his own power. Historical evidence supports that he fathered children with Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman, in a relationship shaped by slavery's coercion.

Ask:
- Why must this be central, not hidden?
- Can true words judge the person who wrote them?
- What is the danger of believing principles only in theory?

### Afternoon: Final Project (45-60 min)

Choose one:

**Ages 8-10:** Draw Jefferson writing the Declaration and a second picture showing “true actions.” Label both.

**Ages 11-14:** Write a one-page answer: “What did Jefferson teach about liberty, and where did he fail?”

**Ages 15-18:** Write a short essay: “Jefferson's principles were greater than Jefferson's practice.” Include the Declaration, education or religious liberty, and slavery.

---

## Optional Weekend Extensions

- Read Jefferson's epitaph and discuss why he chose those three achievements.
- Compare Jefferson with Washington: words and principle versus restraint and precedent.
- Research the University of Virginia Rotunda and explain how architecture can express an educational idea.
- Read a short Monticello biography of one enslaved person connected to Jefferson's household.

---

## Parent Closing Script

“Jefferson shows us that words can matter enormously. His words about equality and liberty helped form America and gave later generations a standard for justice. He also shows us that words are not enough. He enslaved people, and that contradiction must be told truthfully. From Jefferson we learn to love liberty, learning, and conscience—and to make sure our true words become true actions.”
