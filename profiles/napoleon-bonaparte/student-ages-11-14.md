# Napoleon Bonaparte: Student Lesson (Ages 11-14)
## Strategy, Order, and Ambition

---

## The Essential Question

> How can a man's greatest strengths become dangerous?

Napoleon is one of history's clearest examples of disciplined genius joined to dangerous ambition.

---

## An Outsider Formed by Discipline

Napoleon was born in Corsica in 1769, soon after France acquired the island. At age nine he was sent to school in France. He often felt like an outsider: Corsican, not fully French; poor compared with many classmates; serious and intense.

Military school gave him discipline. Artillery taught mathematics, timing, terrain, and concentration. Napoleon learned that force must be applied at the decisive point.

---

## Revolution Opens the Door

The French Revolution weakened the old aristocratic system. Men of ability could rise quickly if they were useful.

Napoleon first became famous at Toulon in 1793, where his artillery plan helped drive out British forces. In 1796, he commanded the Army of Italy and won a series of brilliant victories.

He moved fast, divided his enemies, inspired his soldiers, and used victory as propaganda.

---

## Order After Chaos

In 1799 Napoleon helped overthrow the weak Directory and became First Consul. Many French people accepted him because they wanted order after years of revolution, terror, and instability.

He strengthened administration, supported the Civil Code, founded schools called lycées, and rewarded talent. These were real achievements.

But order came with control. Napoleon censored newspapers, weakened political freedom, and concentrated power in himself.

---

## From Consul to Emperor

In 1804 Napoleon crowned himself Emperor of the French.

This moment reveals the problem. Napoleon claimed to preserve the Revolution's achievements, especially merit and legal equality. But he also created a new monarchy centered on himself.

The servant of France became the master of France.

---

## Overreach

Napoleon won many victories, but each victory created new enemies and new ambitions. His empire spread across Europe.

In 1812 he invaded Russia. The campaign became a disaster. Distance, cold, hunger, disease, Russian strategy, and supply failure destroyed his army.

He was defeated, exiled, returned briefly, and finally lost at Waterloo in 1815.

---

## Lessons to Keep

1. **Discipline matters.** Napoleon's talent was trained by study and work.
2. **Strategy matters.** Find the decisive point; do not scatter effort.
3. **Order matters.** Laws, schools, and administration can bless a nation.
4. **Liberty matters.** Order without freedom becomes control.
5. **Limits matter.** Ambition that refuses limits eventually meets disaster.

---

## Practice: Strengths and Dangers Chart

Make three columns:

**Strength** | **Good Use** | **Dangerous Use**

Fill in examples:
- Discipline | Mastering a craft | Treating people like machines
- Confidence | Courage in crisis | Refusing correction
- Order | Clear laws and schools | Censorship and control
- Ambition | Pursuing excellence | Seeking domination
- Speed | Acting decisively | Rushing into overreach

Write five sentences answering:

> Which of Napoleon's strengths became most dangerous, and why?

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Why did the French Revolution create opportunities for Napoleon?
2. How did artillery shape the way he thought?
3. Why did people accept Napoleon's strong rule?
4. What is the difference between order and liberty?
5. Why was the invasion of Russia such a serious mistake?

---

## Closing Lesson

Napoleon's life shows that strengths are not automatically virtues. Discipline, confidence, and ambition must be governed by justice, humility, and limits.
