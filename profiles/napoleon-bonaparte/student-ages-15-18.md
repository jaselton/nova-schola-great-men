# Napoleon Bonaparte: Student Lesson (Ages 15-18)
## Revolution, Empire, and the Problem of Power

---

## The Essential Question

> Was Napoleon the heir of the French Revolution or its betrayer?

The honest answer is mixed. Napoleon preserved several revolutionary achievements: legal equality for male citizens, careers open to talent, secular administration, and the destruction of many feudal privileges. But he betrayed political liberty through censorship, personal rule, dynastic ambition, and war.

He is therefore not a simple hero or villain. He is a study in power.

---

## Formation: Outsider, Officer, Reader

Napoleon's Corsican childhood mattered. He entered French institutions as an outsider and carried both resentment and ambition. His military education, especially in artillery, trained him to think mathematically: terrain, timing, logistics, concentration, and morale.

He read widely and worked intensely. His early rise was not accidental. The Revolution created opportunity, but Napoleon was ready for it.

---

## Military Genius

Napoleon's military method joined speed, deception, corps organization, decisive concentration, and psychological pressure. He forced opponents to respond to his tempo.

At his best, he saw the hinge of a situation. At Toulon, the key was artillery position. In Italy, the key was dividing enemy forces and defeating them separately. At Austerlitz, the key was inviting weakness and then striking the center.

This is strategic imagination: not merely fighting hard, but understanding where action matters most.

---

## The Consulate: Order and Institution

After the coup of 18 Brumaire in 1799, Napoleon became First Consul. France was weary of revolutionary instability. Napoleon offered competence.

His regime created or strengthened durable institutions:

- Centralized administration and prefects
- The Bank of France
- The Concordat with the Catholic Church
- Lycées for forming officials and officers
- The Civil Code of 1804

The Civil Code is essential. It unified law, protected property, strengthened secular legal order, and affirmed equality before law for male citizens. It also subordinated women within the family and did not create a liberal political order.

Napoleon's gift was order. His danger was control.

---

## Empire: The Revolution Consumed by One Man

In 1804 Napoleon crowned himself emperor. This was not merely a ceremony. It revealed a transformation: revolutionary energy had become personal sovereignty.

Napoleon believed his rule secured the Revolution's best results. But he increasingly identified France's good with his own command. Political opposition narrowed. The press was controlled. Family members were placed on thrones. Europe became a chessboard for imperial design.

A student should ask: Can a ruler preserve equality before law while destroying political liberty?

---

## War and Legitimacy

Napoleon's authority depended heavily on victory. This created a dangerous cycle:

1. Victory increased prestige.
2. Prestige justified power.
3. Power required new security arrangements.
4. New arrangements created enemies.
5. Enemies required new wars.

The empire became difficult to separate from war itself.

---

## Russia and the Judgment of Reality

The 1812 invasion of Russia exposed the limits of Napoleonic will. His army could win battles, but it could not force Russia to make peace on his schedule. Geography, supply, disease, winter, and Russian strategy destroyed the Grande Armée.

This is one of history's great lessons in overreach. Tactical genius cannot redeem strategic folly.

---

## Ethical Judgment

Napoleon's greatness is real:

- He rose by discipline and ability.
- He rewarded talent and weakened inherited privilege.
- He built durable institutions.
- He gave France legal and administrative order.
- He remains one of history's most important military commanders.

Napoleon's failures are also real:

- He concentrated power in himself.
- He censored and controlled political life.
- He turned republican energy into empire.
- He made war central to legitimacy.
- He pursued ambition beyond moral and practical limits.

The mature judgment is not admiration or denunciation alone. It is moral analysis.

## Primary Source Practice

Study the principle often associated with Napoleon's France:

> “Careers open to talent.”

Answer:

1. Why would this principle appeal after aristocratic privilege?
2. How did Napoleon embody it personally?
3. How could a meritocratic system still become authoritarian?

Study the traditional saying attributed to Napoleon before the Battle of the Pyramids:

> “From the summit of these pyramids, forty centuries look down upon you.”

Answer:

1. How does this use history to inspire soldiers?
2. What does it reveal about Napoleon's imagination?
3. When does rhetoric become manipulation?

---

## Comparative Seminar

Compare Napoleon with one of the following:

**Alexander the Great**  
Conquest, speed, charisma, and the inability to build a stable succession.

**George Washington**  
The commander who surrendered power versus the commander who crowned himself.

**Frederick the Great**  
Military statecraft, administrative order, and the burden of monarchy.

**Thomas Jefferson**  
The Louisiana Purchase as a direct result of Napoleon's imperial calculations.

Prepare a five-minute answer:

> What kind of greatness becomes dangerous when it refuses limits?

---

## Essay Prompt

Write 800-1200 words:

**“Napoleon preserved the order of the French Revolution while betraying its liberty.”**

Your essay should include:
- The opportunities created by the Revolution
- Napoleon's military and administrative genius
- The Civil Code
- Censorship, empire, or dynastic ambition
- Russia or Waterloo as the consequence of overreach
- A conclusion about power and limits

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Was Napoleon more revolutionary or monarchical?
2. Can legal equality coexist with political unfreedom?
3. Why did post-revolutionary France accept strong rule?
4. How did Napoleon's legitimacy depend on victory?
5. What does Russia teach about the limits of willpower?

---

## Closing Lesson

Napoleon shows that greatness requires more than ability. It requires moral government of the self.

His life should make students admire discipline, strategy, and institution-building—but fear ambition that cannot kneel before truth.
