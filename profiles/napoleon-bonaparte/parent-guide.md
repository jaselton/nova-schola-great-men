# Parent Guide: How to Teach Napoleon Bonaparte

---

## Why Napoleon Belongs in the Curriculum

Napoleon belongs in the Great Men sequence because he forces students to think seriously about talent, order, power, ambition, and limits.

He was one of history's most gifted commanders and one of modern Europe's most consequential rulers. He rose through merit, restored order after revolutionary chaos, supported the Civil Code, reorganized administration, and shaped Europe. He also censored opposition, crowned himself emperor, filled Europe with war, and pursued ambition until catastrophe.

He is the necessary contrast to George Washington: Washington gave power back; Napoleon gathered it into himself.

---

## Core Formation Aim

Teach Napoleon as a model of **disciplined genius under the judgment of moral limits**.

The child should leave this study understanding:

- Talent must be trained.
- Order and law are real goods.
- Merit is better than inherited privilege.
- Power must be limited.
- Ambition without humility becomes destructive.

---

## What to Emphasize by Age

### Ages 8-10

Keep the story concrete.

Emphasize:
- Napoleon was a serious student and young officer.
- He practiced, studied maps, and learned artillery.
- He became powerful because he was disciplined and brave.
- He wanted too much power and became emperor.
- Talent must obey truth.

Avoid making war glamorous. Focus on practice, pride, and limits.

### Ages 11-14

Introduce the tension between order and liberty.

Emphasize:
- The French Revolution opened careers to ability.
- Napoleon brought order after chaos.
- The Civil Code and schools were real achievements.
- Censorship and empire were serious dangers.
- Russia shows the cost of overreach.

This age can understand how strengths become dangerous.

### Ages 15-18

Teach Napoleon as a political and moral problem.

Emphasize:
- He both preserved and betrayed revolutionary principles.
- Legal equality is not the same as political liberty.
- Meritocracy can serve authoritarian power.
- Military legitimacy tends to require continued victory.
- Washington is the crucial comparison.

Older students should wrestle with the question: Was Napoleon a builder of modern order or a destroyer through ambition?

---

## Virtues to Draw Out

### Discipline

Napoleon's early rise came through study, technical competence, and relentless preparation.

### Strategic Imagination

He could identify the decisive point in a campaign or institution.

### Energy

His capacity for work and organization was extraordinary.

### Merit

He favored ability and usefulness over inherited status.

### Institution-Building

His legal, educational, financial, and administrative reforms outlasted many military victories.

---

## Risks to Name Clearly

### Ambition Without Limit

Napoleon's desire for greatness became a desire for domination.

### Pride

Repeated success made correction harder to hear.

### Control

His love of order narrowed liberty.

### Glory Addiction

Victory became a source of legitimacy, which made peace harder.

### Overreach

Russia shows what happens when willpower ignores reality.

---

## Common Teaching Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Teaching Napoleon Only as a Military Genius

This turns him into a tactics lesson and hides the moral problem of power.

### Mistake 2: Teaching Napoleon Only as a Tyrant

This misses the real institutional achievements: the Civil Code, administration, education, and meritocratic opportunity.

### Mistake 3: Romanticizing Conquest

Students should not confuse battlefield brilliance with moral greatness.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring the French Revolution

Without the Revolution, Napoleon's rise and appeal make little sense.

### Mistake 5: Skipping Washington

The Washington comparison is essential. Both were victorious generals. One surrendered power; the other crowned himself.

---

## Discussion Prompts for the Family

- What is the difference between confidence and pride?
- Why do people sometimes choose order over liberty?
- Can a good law come from a flawed ruler?
- When does ambition help a person grow?
- When does ambition become dangerous?
- Why is giving up power sometimes greater than winning power?

---

## Simple Assessment

Ask the student to explain Napoleon in three sentences:

1. One sentence about his disciplined rise.
2. One sentence about his achievements in law or administration.
3. One sentence about his ambition and fall.

A strong answer might say:

> Napoleon rose from Corsican outsider to ruler of France through discipline, military genius, and the opportunities of the Revolution. He brought order through laws, schools, and administration. But his ambition became empire and war, and his refusal to accept limits led to Russia, Waterloo, and exile.

---

## Parent Takeaway

Teach Napoleon with admiration and warning together. Let students respect his discipline, energy, strategy, and institution-building. But make the central lesson plain: greatness without self-government becomes dangerous.
