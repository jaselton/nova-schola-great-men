# Erasmus
## The Scholar Who Tried to Reform Christendom with Words

**Born:** October 27 or 28, 1469, Rotterdam  
**Died:** July 12, 1536, Basel  
**Era:** Renaissance / Northern Renaissance / Reformation Era  
**Domain:** Christian Humanism, Biblical Scholarship, Education, Satire, Peace Writing

---

## The One-Minute Story

Erasmus was one of the most famous scholars in Europe at the beginning of the Reformation.

He was born in Rotterdam, educated by teachers shaped by the *Devotio Moderna*, and pushed into religious life before finding his true vocation as a wandering scholar. He traveled through Europe, made friends with men like Thomas More and John Colet, worked with great printers, and became a teacher to thousands through his books.

Erasmus believed Christian renewal should begin with learning, Scripture, clear language, inward devotion, and peace. He wrote *The Praise of Folly*, a brilliant satire of human vanity. He produced the 1516 Greek-Latin New Testament, helping Europe return to the biblical sources. He wrote educational works because he believed children could be formed toward wisdom and virtue.

But Erasmus lived in an age when moderation became difficult. Protestants thought he saw the problems but lacked courage. Catholics suspected his criticisms helped unleash rebellion. He wanted reform without schism, peace without cowardice, and learning without pride.

He teaches students that words matter, sources matter, education matters, and wit must serve charity.

---

## Why He Matters for Your Child

**Erasmus teaches:**
- Why careful reading and clear language matter
- How education can become moral formation
- The courage to return to original sources
- The value of peace, moderation, and intellectual independence

**But also warns against:**
- Cleverness without charity
- Criticism that does not build
- Caution that becomes evasion
- Scholarship detached from institutions and action

## Formation Signature

### Virtues (What to Imitate)
- **Learned piety** — joining serious study with Christian moral purpose
- **Eloquence** — using language to clarify, delight, and correct
- **Intellectual independence** — refusing to let factions own his conscience
- **Peaceableness** — preferring persuasion to violence and party rage
- **Source discipline** — returning to texts, languages, and evidence

### Risks (What to Guard Against)
- **Over-caution** — delaying too long when action is needed
- **Sharp satire** — exposing folly without enough tenderness
- **Rootlessness** — independence without durable institutional responsibility
- **Elitism** — letting literary refinement become contempt for ordinary people

### How He Was Formed
- **Rotterdam and orphanhood** → vulnerability, ambition, and hunger for independence
- **Deventer and the Brethren of the Common Life** → inward devotion and early humanist learning
- **Steyn monastery** → religious discipline mixed with dissatisfaction
- **Paris tutoring and study** → necessity turned into educational authorship
- **England and Italy** → friendships, Greek learning, printers, and the European republic of letters
- **Basel printing world** → scholarship multiplied through books

## Quick Practice Ideas

**Ages 8-10:**
- Copy one sentence beautifully and explain what it means
- Practice correcting someone kindly
- Memorize: “Use words to tell the truth with love.”

**Ages 11-14:**
- Compare a harsh joke with a helpful satire
- Make an *ad fontes* chart: original source, later summary, what changed
- Debate: Is refusing to choose a side brave or weak?

**Ages 15-18:**
- Analyze the preface to *The Praise of Folly*
- Essay: “Can education reform society without institutions?”
- Seminar: Erasmus vs. Luther on reform, freedom, and certainty
