# Erasmus: One-Week Lesson Plan

---

## Overview

**Target:** All ages (select activities appropriate to your student's level)  
**Time commitment:** 45-60 minutes/day  
**Structure:** 5 days, Monday-Friday  
**Goal:** Introduce Erasmus's life, Christian humanism, love of sources, satire, biblical scholarship, peace writing, and the ambiguity of moderation

---

## Before You Begin

### Materials Needed
- Notebook or binder
- Blank paper for charts and reflection
- Colored pencils or markers
- Access to a short excerpt from *The Praise of Folly*
- Optional: a page image from an early printed book or Erasmus's Greek New Testament
- Optional: a short proverb or adage for copywork

### Choose Your Age Band
- **Ages 8-10:** Focus on good words, careful reading, and kind correction
- **Ages 11-14:** Add *ad fontes*, satire, peace, and reform
- **Ages 15-18:** Add Christian humanism, textual criticism, Erasmus vs. Luther, and institutional limits

---

## Monday: The Story

### Morning: Read Together (20-30 min)

**Ages 8-10:** Read the [student lesson ages 8-10](./student-ages-8-10.md) together  
**Ages 11-14:** Read the [student lesson ages 11-14](./student-ages-11-14.md) independently or aloud  
**Ages 15-18:** Assign the [student lesson ages 15-18](./student-ages-15-18.md) as independent reading

**Pause to discuss:**
- Why did Erasmus care so much about words?
- What does it mean to go back to the sources?
- Why might both sides mistrust a moderate reformer?

---

### Afternoon: Erasmus Map (20 min)

Draw Erasmus in the center of a page. Around him, add:

- Rotterdam
- Deventer
- Paris
- England
- Italy
- Basel
- Thomas More
- Printers
- Greek New Testament
- *The Praise of Folly*

Draw lines showing how travel, friendship, books, and printers made Erasmus influential.

**Reflection:**  
Erasmus did not rule a country. How did he still shape Europe?

---

## Tuesday: Good Words and Sources

### Morning: Memory Work (5 min)

Younger students memorize:

> Use words to tell the truth with love.

Older students memorize:

> Before you argue loudly, read carefully.

---

### Afternoon: Ad Fontes Exercise (30-40 min)

Choose a short passage, proverb, news claim, or historical quotation.

Make a chart:

1. What people say it means
2. What the original source actually says
3. What changed in the retelling
4. Why the difference matters

**Ages 8-10:** Use one sentence from a storybook.  
**Ages 11-14:** Use a quotation or historical claim.  
**Ages 15-18:** Use a translated passage and compare two translations.

---

## Wednesday: Satire and Folly

### Morning: Read a Short Excerpt (20-30 min)

Read a short section from the preface or opening of *The Praise of Folly*.

Explain:
- Erasmus dedicates the work to Thomas More
- Folly speaks in her own voice
- The humor has a serious moral purpose

**Discussion questions:**
- Why might humor correct someone better than direct accusation?
- When does satire become cruel?
- What folly in yourself can you laugh at humbly?

---

### Afternoon: Helpful Satire Practice (20-30 min)

Choose one foolish habit:

- Bragging
- Interrupting
- Pretending to know everything
- Laziness
- Chasing popularity

Write or draw two versions:

1. Cruel mockery
2. Helpful satire

Explain why the second is better.

Older students should add a paragraph on whether satire can be an act of charity.

---

## Thursday: Reform and Peace

### Morning: The Reformation Crisis (30 min)

Tell the story at the right level:

**Ages 8-10:** Erasmus wanted people to read carefully, love truth, and make peace.  
**Ages 11-14:** Erasmus criticized Church abuses but did not join Luther's movement; he feared division and conflict.  
**Ages 15-18:** Discuss Erasmus's Christian humanism, Luther's Reformation, and the free-will controversy.

**Discussion questions:**
- Is peace always possible?
- When is refusing to join a side wise?
- When can refusing to choose become irresponsible?

---

### Afternoon: Moderation Debate (25-30 min)

Topic:

> Erasmus was right to remain independent.

**Arguments for:**
- He resisted factional rage.
- He defended peace.
- He saw dangers on both sides.

**Arguments against:**
- He criticized problems but did not lead a durable reform.
- He disappointed people who wanted clear action.
- His moderation could look like hesitation.

After debate, each student writes:

> Erasmus's moderation was admirable because ___. It was limited because ___.

---

## Friday: Application and Synthesis

### Morning: Build with Words (30 min)

Design a plan to improve one small part of family, school, church, or community life through better words.

Include:
- What problem needs better words?
- What source should be checked?
- What correction must be made kindly?
- What habit or institution would make the change last?

**Ages 8-10:** Write three kind sentences you can use this week.  
**Ages 11-14:** Create a one-page “Better Words Plan.”  
**Ages 15-18:** Write a one-page proposal with an institutional safeguard.

---

### Afternoon: Final Reflection (20-30 min)

Choose one prompt:

**Ages 8-10:**  
Draw Erasmus with a book and write: “Words should serve truth and love.”

**Ages 11-14:**  
Write one paragraph: “What Erasmus teaches me about reading before arguing.”

**Ages 15-18:**  
Write an essay outline: “Can education reform society without institutions?”

---

## Optional Extensions

### Literature
Read selected passages from *The Praise of Folly* or *Colloquies*.

### History
Place Erasmus on a timeline with Lorenzo de' Medici, Leonardo da Vinci, Luther, and Charles V.

### Language Arts
Collect five proverbs in the spirit of Erasmus's *Adages*. Explain what each one teaches.

### Theology / Philosophy
Compare Erasmus's “philosophy of Christ” with Augustine's restless heart or Anselm's faith seeking understanding.

---

## Week-End Synthesis

By the end of the week, students should understand that Erasmus was a scholar of reform. He believed books, words, sources, education, satire, and peace could renew Christian life.

They should also understand the warning: criticism must build, wit must be charitable, and moderation must not avoid responsibility.

**Final sentence to remember:**

> Before you argue loudly, read carefully — then use words to build what truth requires.
