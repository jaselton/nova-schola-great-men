# George Washington
## The Man Who Gave Power Back

**Born:** February 22, 1732, Westmoreland County, Virginia
**Died:** December 14, 1799, Mount Vernon, Virginia
**Era:** Modern / American Founding
**Domain:** Military Command, Republican Statesmanship, Civic Virtue, Constitutional Government

---

## The One-Minute Story

George Washington was the soldier-statesman who helped teach a new republic what power was for.

As a young Virginian, he wanted honor and advancement. He learned surveying, manners, frontier hardship, and military command. He also learned from failure. Early defeats in the French and Indian War humbled him and trained him for larger responsibility.

When the American colonies rebelled against Britain, Washington became commander of the Continental Army. He did not win by constant brilliance. He won by endurance: keeping the army together, learning from mistakes, and refusing to let the cause collapse.

His greatest moments came after victory. He resigned his military command instead of becoming a ruler. He helped preside over the Constitutional Convention. As first president, he established habits of constitutional government, then retired after two terms.

Washington teaches students that true greatness is not merely taking command. It is commanding yourself, serving a cause larger than yourself, and giving power back when duty is done.

He must also be taught honestly: Washington enslaved people at Mount Vernon. His virtues were real, and so was this grave contradiction.

## Why He Matters for Your Child

**Washington teaches:**
- How self-command makes leadership possible
- Why endurance can matter more than brilliance
- That public office is a trust, not a possession
- How one person's example can shape a whole republic

**But also warns against:**
- Ambition disguised as honor
- Reputation becoming too important
- Moral blindness inside a respected culture
- Praising liberty while tolerating injustice

## Formation Signature

### Virtues (What to Imitate)
- **Self-command** — governing temper, pride, fear, and ambition
- **Perseverance** — refusing to quit when the army and cause were fragile
- **Prudence** — learning when to fight, retreat, wait, or act boldly
- **Public duty** — treating authority as service
- **Restraint** — surrendering power rather than clinging to it

### Risks (What to Guard Against)
- **Status hunger** — caring too much about reputation
- **Emotional distance** — dignity that becomes coldness
- **Caution without imagination** — prudence slipping into hesitation
- **Moral compromise** — participating in injustice while speaking of liberty

### How He Was Formed
- **Virginia childhood** → manners, ambition, loss of father, practical education
- **Surveying** → discipline, terrain, endurance, responsibility
- **French and Indian War** → failure, courage, military apprenticeship
- **Revolutionary command** → patience under pressure and political judgment
- **Retirement from power** → republican self-command made visible

## Quick Practice Ideas

**Ages 8-10:**
- Draw Washington resigning his command
- Practice a “self-command moment” by waiting calmly before speaking
- Memorize: “Power is for service, not showing off.”

**Ages 11-14:**
- Make a timeline from Fort Necessity to the presidency
- Compare Trenton with Valley Forge: bold action and patient endurance
- Discuss: Why is giving power back difficult?

**Ages 15-18:**
- Analyze Washington's resignation address or Farewell Address
- Essay: “Was Washington's restraint his greatest achievement?”
- Seminar: How should we judge a great founder who enslaved people?
