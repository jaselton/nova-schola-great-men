# George Washington: One-Week Lesson Plan
## The Man Who Gave Power Back

---

## Overview

This one-week study teaches George Washington through the theme of self-command: learning from failure, enduring hardship, leading under pressure, and surrendering power when duty is complete.

**Formation Aim:** Students should understand that true leadership requires restraint, perseverance, and moral honesty.

---

## Before You Begin

### Materials Needed

- Student lesson for the appropriate age band
- Notebook or binder page for Washington
- Map of the American colonies / Revolutionary War
- Short excerpt from Washington's resignation address
- Short excerpt from the Farewell Address
- Optional: image of Washington resigning his commission

### Choose Your Age Band

- **Ages 8-10:** Focus on story, courage, self-command, and giving power back.
- **Ages 11-14:** Add formation through failure and the difficulty of the Revolution.
- **Ages 15-18:** Add republican virtue, Cincinnatus, constitutional precedent, and slavery.

---

## Monday: The Story

### Morning: Read Together (20-30 min)

Read the profile overview and the appropriate student lesson.

Ask:
- What kind of man was Washington at the beginning?
- What made his job difficult?
- Why is giving power back important?

### Afternoon: Start a Washington Notebook (15-20 min)

Create a page with four boxes:

1. **Young Washington** — surveyor, ambitious, learning honor
2. **General Washington** — endurance, mistakes, Trenton, Valley Forge
3. **President Washington** — precedents, dignity, duty
4. **The Hard Truth** — slavery and moral contradiction

Younger students may draw pictures. Older students should write three bullet points per box.

---

## Tuesday: Formation Through Failure

### Morning: Memory Work (5 min)

Copy or recite:

> A great leader must first learn to lead himself.

Older students may add:

> Ambition becomes noble only when disciplined by duty.

### Afternoon: Fort Necessity Exercise (25-35 min)

Explain Washington's early defeat at Fort Necessity.

Discuss:
- What did Washington lack as a young officer?
- Why can failure be a better teacher than easy success?
- What should a student do after making a serious mistake?

Written response:

**Ages 8-10:** Draw “Washington learning from failure.”
**Ages 11-14:** Write five sentences on what failure can teach.
**Ages 15-18:** Explain how early failure may have prepared Washington for later command.

---

## Wednesday: Endurance and Boldness

### Morning: Map the War (30 min)

Find and mark:
- Boston
- New York
- Delaware River
- Trenton
- Valley Forge
- Yorktown
- Mount Vernon

Discuss how distance, weather, supplies, and morale affected the war.

### Afternoon: Two Scenes (25-35 min)

Compare two moments:

**Trenton:** Bold action at a desperate time.
**Valley Forge:** Patient endurance during suffering.

Ask:
- Which moment required more courage?
- Can waiting be brave?
- When should a leader act boldly?

Older students should write a paragraph comparing boldness and endurance.

---

## Thursday: Power and Restraint

### Morning: Primary Source Copywork (15-25 min)

Copy Washington's resignation line:

> “I here offer my Commission, and take my leave of all the employments of public life.”

Ask:
- What power was Washington giving back?
- Why would this surprise people in history?
- How does this protect a republic?

### Afternoon: The Presidency as Precedent (25-40 min)

Explain that Washington was the first president, so his actions taught later presidents what the office meant.

Make a list of precedents:
- Cabinet consultation
- Respect for constitutional limits
- Peaceful transfer of power
- Retirement after two terms

Older students: connect these precedents to the idea that office is a trust.

---

## Friday: Moral Honesty and Synthesis

### Morning: The Hard Truth (20-30 min)

Teach plainly and calmly:

Washington enslaved people at Mount Vernon. His will arranged for the freedom of the enslaved people he personally owned, but this came late and did not free everyone held in bondage at Mount Vernon because some were Custis dower slaves.

Ask:
- Why must we tell this truth?
- Can a person have real virtues and real sins?
- How do we avoid both hero-worship and cynicism?

### Afternoon: Final Project (45-60 min)

Choose one:

**Ages 8-10:** Draw Washington crossing the Delaware and Washington giving power back. Label both pictures.

**Ages 11-14:** Write a one-page answer: “Why was Washington trusted with power?” Include one hard truth.

**Ages 15-18:** Write a short essay: “Washington's restraint was a constitutional act.” Include the Revolution, resignation, presidency, and slavery.

---

## Optional Weekend Extensions

- Read excerpts from the Farewell Address.
- Compare Washington with Frederick the Great or Napoleon.
- Research Cincinnatus and explain why Americans used that comparison.
- Visit the Mount Vernon website for primary-source images and educational materials.

---

## Parent Closing Script

“Washington was not great because he was perfect. He was great because, at crucial moments, he governed himself while holding power. He endured defeat, served a difficult cause, and gave authority back when he could have tried to keep it. We also tell the truth that he enslaved people, because real history requires moral honesty. From Washington we learn self-command, restraint, and the duty to judge greatness by justice.”
