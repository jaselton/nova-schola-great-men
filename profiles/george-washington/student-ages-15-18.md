# George Washington: Student Lesson (Ages 15-18)
## Republican Virtue and the Limits of Greatness

---

## The Essential Question

> Was Washington's greatest achievement winning power, using power, or surrendering power?

Washington's importance lies not only in the offices he held, but in the precedents he set. He embodied a form of republican virtue: personal ambition disciplined by public duty.

---

## Washington and the Problem of Power

The American Revolution created a dangerous problem. A war for liberty required a military commander with unusual authority. But military authority can threaten the liberty it claims to defend.

Washington's answer was character and precedent. He accepted command, endured criticism, held the army together, and then resigned. He later accepted the presidency and then retired.

His career asks a serious political question: Can institutions remain free if leaders lack self-command?

---

## Formation: Honor Disciplined into Duty

Washington was ambitious. He desired rank, reputation, land, and recognition. This is not incidental to his character; it is part of the story.

In the Virginia world that formed him, honor mattered. Public reputation could discipline conduct, but it could also feed vanity. Washington's development was the gradual conversion of honor-seeking into service.

He learned from surveying, frontier hardship, military failure, plantation management, legislative service, and war. He became great not by lacking ambition, but by placing ambition under duty.

---

## Strategic Greatness: Survival Before Victory

Washington was not an undefeated battlefield genius. His early Revolutionary campaigns included misjudgment and defeat. But his strategic insight was deeper: the Continental Army had to survive.

He learned to avoid annihilation, preserve morale, maintain political legitimacy, and coordinate with allies. Trenton displayed boldness; Valley Forge displayed endurance; Yorktown displayed coalition warfare and strategic patience.

The Revolution was won not merely by heroic moments, but by durable command.

---

## The Cincinnatus Pattern

Washington was often compared with Cincinnatus, the Roman figure who accepted emergency authority and then returned to private life.

The comparison matters because Washington lived in a world that knew military glory could become tyranny. His resignation at Annapolis in 1783 was therefore not ceremonial. It was a constitutional act before the Constitution existed: military power subordinated itself to civil authority.

His two-term retirement deepened the pattern. He taught the presidency that dignity includes limits.

---

## The Slavery Contradiction

No serious study of Washington can avoid slavery.

Washington enslaved people and benefited from their labor. He also served a revolution that spoke in the language of liberty. His will provided for the freedom of enslaved people he personally owned after Martha Washington's death, and Martha freed them earlier. But this late act does not erase the reality of his long participation in slavery or the limits imposed by Custis dower property.

This contradiction should not be treated as a footnote. It forces students to practice moral judgment: to see public greatness and grave injustice in the same life without collapsing either truth.

---

## The Ethical Judgment

Washington's greatness is real:

- He held together the Revolutionary cause.
- He subordinated military authority to civil government.
- He gave the presidency precedents of dignity, restraint, and peaceful transfer.
- He warned against faction and foreign dependency.

Washington's failures are also real:

- He participated in slavery.
- He was slow to act against an injustice bound up with his own household and wealth.
- His dignity could become emotional distance.
- His desire for honor required discipline.

A mature judgment does not need a flawless hero. It needs the truth.

## Primary Source Practice

Read these lines from Washington's resignation address:

> “I here offer my Commission, and take my leave of all the employments of public life.”

Answer:

1. Why would this sentence have mattered in 1783?
2. What danger did it help prevent?
3. How does it compare with rulers or generals who cling to power?

Now read this line from the Farewell Address:

> “Promote then, as an object of primary importance, institutions for the general diffusion of knowledge.”

Answer:

1. Why would republican government require public knowledge?
2. What happens when public opinion is powerful but poorly educated?
3. How does this connect to Nova Schola's purpose?

---

## Comparative Seminar

Compare Washington with one of the following:

**Frederick the Great**
Efficient monarchy and state service versus republican restraint.

**Napoleon Bonaparte**
Military genius and personal domination versus military leadership restrained by civil authority.

**Marcus Aurelius**
Self-command in imperial office versus self-command in republican founding.

Prepare a five-minute seminar answer: “What kind of self-command did each figure possess, and where did each fall short?”

---

## Essay Prompt

Write 800-1200 words:

**“George Washington's greatness lay less in winning the American Revolution than in teaching Americans how power should be limited.”**

Your essay should include:
- One example from the Revolutionary War
- One example from 1783
- One example from the presidency
- One paragraph on slavery and moral contradiction
- A conclusion about republican virtue

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Is republican government more dependent on laws or character?
2. How did Washington's personal reputation strengthen public institutions?
3. Why is voluntary retirement from power politically powerful?
4. How should students judge a founder whose liberty language coexisted with slaveholding?
5. What dangers arise when a nation needs an “indispensable man”?

---

## Closing Lesson

Washington's life reveals the moral architecture of republican leadership. Institutions need laws, but they also need examples. Washington gave Americans an example of command without seizure, authority without monarchy, and retirement without collapse.

Yet the same life also reveals how a society can praise liberty while denying it. The student who studies Washington well should become neither cynical nor naive, but morally serious.
