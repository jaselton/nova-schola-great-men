# Parent Guide: How to Teach George Washington

---

## Why Washington Belongs in the Curriculum

George Washington belongs in the Great Men sequence because he demonstrates the virtue of restraint at the founding of a republic.

Many leaders become famous by taking power. Washington became great by taking power reluctantly, using it under discipline, and giving it back. His resignation of military command and retirement after two presidential terms are central acts of republican statesmanship.

He also belongs because he must be taught truthfully. Washington's slaveholding is not a minor blemish. It is a grave contradiction. A serious curriculum should help children admire real virtue without hiding real injustice.

---

## Core Formation Aim

Teach Washington as a model of **self-command in public duty**.

The child should leave this study understanding:

- Leadership begins with governing oneself.
- Endurance often matters more than brilliance.
- Public office is a trust, not a possession.
- Restraint can be more glorious than conquest.
- Historical greatness must be judged honestly.

---

## What to Emphasize by Age

### Ages 8-10

Keep the story concrete.

Emphasize:
- Washington leading tired soldiers
- Crossing the Delaware
- Not giving up during hard times
- Giving power back after the war
- The simple wrongness of slavery

Avoid long constitutional theory. Use the phrase: “A great leader must first lead himself.”

### Ages 11-14

Introduce formation through failure.

Emphasize:
- His youthful desire for honor
- Fort Necessity and military learning
- The difficulty of holding the army together
- The difference between courage and self-command
- Honest discussion of slavery as contradiction

This age can understand that a person may have virtues in one area and serious blindness in another.

### Ages 15-18

Teach Washington as a study in republican virtue.

Emphasize:
- Civil-military relations
- Cincinnatus and voluntary retirement
- Constitutional precedent
- The Farewell Address
- Slavery, liberty, and moral judgment

Students should wrestle with whether Washington's greatest contribution was military, institutional, or moral.

---

## Virtues to Draw Out

### Self-Command

Washington's dignity was not merely style. It was discipline over temper, fear, ambition, and pride.

### Perseverance

The Revolution survived because Washington endured repeated defeats, shortages, criticism, and uncertainty.

### Prudence

He learned when to retreat, when to risk, when to wait, and when to act.

### Public Duty

He treated authority as something entrusted to him for a purpose larger than himself.

### Restraint

His most famous acts after victory were acts of limitation: resigning command and retiring from office.

---

## Risks to Name Clearly

### Ambition for Reputation

Washington cared deeply about honor. This helped discipline him, but it could also tempt him toward image management.

### Emotional Reserve

His dignity could become distance. Students should not confuse self-command with coldness.

### Over-Caution

Prudence is good; fear disguised as prudence is not. Washington sometimes faced criticism for caution.

### Slavery

Name this plainly. Washington enslaved people. His later emancipation provision was significant but late and incomplete.

---

## Common Teaching Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Making Washington Flawless

Children do not need marble statues. They need truthful examples.

### Mistake 2: Reducing Washington Only to Slavery

His slaveholding must be taught clearly, but it does not erase his real role in establishing republican precedent.

### Mistake 3: Treating Restraint as Weakness

Students often admire bold conquest. Washington shows that refusing domination can be a higher strength.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring His Failures

Early military failure is essential to the lesson. It shows formation through humiliation and learning.

### Mistake 5: Teaching the Presidency as Inevitable

Washington's actions set precedents. The office could have developed differently.

---

## Discussion Prompts for the Family

- Why is it hard for powerful people to stop using power?
- What is the difference between wanting honor and wanting applause?
- When is retreat wise rather than cowardly?
- Why did Washington's resignation matter so much?
- How should we speak truthfully about a person who did great good and serious wrong?
- What responsibilities in our home require self-command?

---

## Simple Assessment

Ask the student to explain Washington in three sentences:

1. One sentence about his formation.
2. One sentence about his greatest public virtue.
3. One sentence about his moral contradiction.

A strong answer might say:

> Washington was formed by ambition, frontier hardship, military failure, and long responsibility. His greatest public virtue was self-command, especially his willingness to give power back. But he also enslaved people, which makes his life a serious lesson in both greatness and moral contradiction.

---

## Parent Takeaway

Teach Washington neither as a saint nor as a fraud. Teach him as a man whose disciplined restraint helped make republican government believable, and whose slaveholding shows why even honored founders must stand under moral judgment.
