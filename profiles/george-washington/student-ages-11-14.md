# George Washington: Student Lesson (Ages 11-14)
## Endurance, Honor, and the American Revolution

---

## The Essential Question

> Why did Washington become the one man Americans trusted with power?

Washington was not trusted because he was perfect. He was trusted because he showed steadiness under pressure and restraint after victory.

---

## A Young Man Seeking Honor

George Washington grew up in Virginia after losing his father at age eleven. He did not receive the deepest classical education of some Founders, but he learned practical skills: surveying, mathematics, land measurement, careful manners, and disciplined conduct.

He wanted to rise in the world. He wanted honor.

That ambition could have become vanity. Over time, hardship trained it into something better: public duty.

---

## Failure as Formation

Washington's early military career included serious failure. In the French and Indian War, he was involved in the Ohio Valley conflict and surrendered at Fort Necessity in 1754.

That was embarrassing. But it taught him.

He learned that courage is not enough. A commander needs supplies, intelligence, patience, and judgment. During Braddock's defeat in 1755, Washington showed courage under fire. He was being formed by both failure and danger.

---

## Commander of a Fragile Army

In 1775, Congress chose Washington to command the Continental Army.

The army was fragile. Soldiers lacked food, clothing, pay, and training. Enlistments expired. Congress often could not provide enough money. The British army was powerful and professional.

Washington made mistakes, especially in the New York campaign. But he kept the army alive. That was essential. If the army disappeared, the Revolution might disappear with it.

His leadership depended on endurance.

---

## Trenton and Valley Forge

Two scenes show Washington's character.

**Trenton:** After defeats in 1776, Washington crossed the Delaware River in winter and won a surprise victory. This showed boldness at the right moment.

**Valley Forge:** During the winter of 1777-1778, the army suffered greatly. Washington held it together while Baron von Steuben helped train the troops. This showed patience and endurance.

A good leader needs both: bold action and patient staying power.

---

## Giving Power Back

After the war, Washington resigned his command to Congress. This mattered enormously.

Many victorious generals in history have kept power. Washington gave it back.

Later, as first president, he again set an example. He served two terms, then retired. This helped teach Americans that the presidency was not a throne.

Washington's greatness was not only that he led. It was that he knew when to stop leading.

---

## The Moral Contradiction

Washington also enslaved people at Mount Vernon.

This must be named clearly. The same man who helped a people claim liberty denied liberty to others. His will arranged for the emancipation of the enslaved people he personally owned, but this came late and did not solve the whole injustice.

Studying Washington honestly means neither pretending he was flawless nor pretending his virtues were unreal.

---

## Lessons to Keep

1. **Ambition must be trained.** Wanting honor is dangerous unless it becomes duty.
2. **Failure can form judgment.** Washington learned from early humiliation.
3. **Endurance can save a cause.** He kept the army alive through long difficulty.
4. **Restraint is strength.** Giving power back can be more impressive than seizing it.
5. **History requires moral honesty.** Real virtues and real injustices can exist in one life.

---

## Practice: Washington's Three Tests

Make three boxes on a page.

**Test 1: Failure**
How did Washington respond to early defeat?

**Test 2: Power**
How did Washington respond after military victory?

**Test 3: Contradiction**
How should we speak truthfully about Washington and slavery?

Write three to five sentences in each box.

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Why might Congress have chosen Washington instead of a louder or more brilliant man?
2. Was Washington's main revolutionary contribution military, political, or moral?
3. Why is self-command important for anyone with authority?
4. What makes slavery such a serious contradiction in Washington's life?
5. How can we honor what is honorable without hiding what is wrong?

---

## Closing Lesson

Washington's life teaches that leadership is not only action. It is endurance, judgment, restraint, and example.

A republic needs leaders who can command others because they have first learned to command themselves.
