# Charlemagne: Student Lesson (Ages 15-18)
## Empire, Reform, and the Moral Limits of Power

---

## The Central Problem

**Can political power form a Christian civilization without violating the freedom and dignity required for true conversion and virtue?**

Charlemagne's life forces this question because his greatness and his danger are inseparable. He preserved learning, built institutions, strengthened schools, and reshaped Europe. He also waged brutal wars and sometimes treated religious unity as something that could be imposed by force.

---

## Historical Context: The Carolingian Project

Charlemagne did not appear out of nowhere. He inherited the Carolingian revolution.

His grandfather, Charles Martel, had become the military master of the Frankish realm. His father, Pepin III, transformed de facto power into kingship by displacing the Merovingians and receiving papal legitimation. This created a new model: Frankish military strength in alliance with Roman Christianity.

Charlemagne inherited that model and expanded it dramatically. He was not merely preserving a kingdom; he was attempting to reorder the West.

The question is how he understood that order.

It was not modern nationalism. It was not secular empire. It was not liberal pluralism. Charlemagne imagined a Christian people under a ruler responsible before God for justice, worship, education, and moral discipline.

---

## Conquest and Integration

Charlemagne's reign was astonishingly active militarily:

- Lombardy was conquered in 774.
- Saxony was subdued only after decades of war.
- Bavaria was absorbed.
- The Avars were defeated.
- The Spanish frontier was contested.

But conquest alone could not hold such a realm. The empire contained different peoples with different laws and customs. Charlemagne often preserved local laws while trying to impose higher unity through Christianity, royal justice, education, and administrative inspection.

This is why his capitularies matter. They are not random regulations. They show a ruler trying to translate moral and religious order into public discipline.

---

## The Carolingian Renaissance as Formation

The term "Carolingian Renaissance" can mislead modern students. This was not primarily a celebration of individual creativity or artistic self-expression. It was a reform of Christian learning.

Charlemagne and his scholars sought:

1. **Correct worship** — The liturgy should be orderly and unified.
2. **Correct texts** — Scripture and patristic writings should be copied accurately.
3. **Correct language** — Clergy needed Latin good enough to understand what they prayed and preached.
4. **Correct morals** — Bishops, abbots, priests, judges, and laypeople were to be disciplined toward Christian conduct.
5. **Correct administration** — Royal agents should expose corruption and enforce justice.

The *Admonitio Generalis* of 789 is the key document. It treats education as public responsibility. The ruler calls bishops and abbots to establish schools and correct books because ignorance damages souls and society.

This is formation at scale.

---

## The Role of Alcuin

Alcuin of York was not merely a court ornament. He supplied intellectual architecture. He represented the Northumbrian scholarly tradition, with its concern for grammar, biblical interpretation, liturgy, and pedagogy.

Charlemagne's genius was not that he personally knew everything. He did not. His genius was that he knew learning mattered and gathered people who could teach.

This is a crucial leadership lesson: the great ruler is not the man who needs no teachers, but the man who can recognize and empower them.

---

## The Moral Crisis: Saxony

The Saxon wars are the central test case for Charlemagne's moral legacy.

The Saxons resisted Frankish control and Christianization. Charlemagne responded with repeated campaigns, destruction of pagan shrines, forced baptisms, deportations, and severe punishments. The massacre at Verden in 782, where the annals report thousands executed, remains the most infamous moment.

Several interpretations are possible:

1. **Political Interpretation:** Saxony was a security threat; harsh suppression was meant to stop rebellion.
2. **Religious Interpretation:** Charlemagne saw paganism as both spiritual error and political disorder.
3. **Civilizational Interpretation:** He believed unity required shared worship and discipline.
4. **Moral Interpretation:** He exceeded justice by treating conversion as conquerable.

The fourth cannot be avoided. A Christian ruler may defend his people and punish violence. But forced conversion confuses external submission with faith. Alcuin himself warned that adults must be instructed before baptism and that faith cannot be compelled by violence.

Here Charlemagne becomes a warning as well as a model.

---

## Coronation and the Problem of Empire

On Christmas Day, 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor in Rome. The event revived imperial dignity in the Latin West, but it also created enduring questions:

- Did the pope make the emperor, or merely recognize him?
- Was this a restoration of Rome or a new Frankish-Christian empire?
- What was the relationship between Western emperor and Byzantine emperor?
- Does Christian civilization require imperial political unity?

Charlemagne reportedly indicated that he would not have entered the church that day had he known the pope's plan. Whether this is literal or diplomatic, it shows the political delicacy of the act.

The coronation mattered because it gave medieval Europe a new imagination: Rome, Christianity, and northern warrior kingship fused into one symbolic order.

---

## The Incompleteness of Charlemagne's Achievement

Charlemagne's empire fractured after his death. This does not make his life a failure, but it clarifies the limits of personal rule.

His reforms had two different legacies:

**Political unity:** fragile, dependent on dynasty, inheritance, and military control.

**Cultural formation:** more durable, preserved in schools, scripts, manuscripts, liturgical reforms, and educational expectations.

The lesson is profound: cultural and educational reforms may outlast political borders. The empire divided; the books remained.

## Practices for Students (Ages 15-18)

### 1. Source Analysis: Einhard
Read the sections of Einhard's *Life of Charlemagne* on Charlemagne's studies and habits.

Write 500 words answering:
- How does Einhard want us to see Charlemagne?
- What virtues does he emphasize?
- What details make Charlemagne human rather than legendary?
- Where might Einhard be idealizing his subject?

---

### 2. The Admonitio as Formation Document
Read excerpts from the *Admonitio Generalis*.

Create a chart:

Command | Problem Addressed | Formation Goal
---|---|---
Establish schools | Clerical ignorance | Literate Christian leadership
Correct books | Textual corruption | Faithful worship and teaching
Discipline clergy | Moral disorder | Trustworthy shepherds

Add at least five entries.

---

### 3. Ethical Debate
Debate the resolution:

> Charlemagne's contribution to Christian civilization outweighs the moral stain of his coercive policies in Saxony.

Rules:
- One side must defend the resolution.
- One side must oppose it.
- Both sides must acknowledge at least one strong point from the other side.
- No side may excuse forced conversion.

---

### 4. Comparative Essay
Choose one comparison:

- Charlemagne and Alexander: conquest and cultural formation
- Charlemagne and Marcus Aurelius: interior virtue vs. institutional reform
- Charlemagne and Augustine: earthly order and the City of God
- Charlemagne and Napoleon: empire, law, education, and ambition

Thesis question:

> Which figure better understood the relationship between power and formation?

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Is Charlemagne best understood as a restorer of Rome, a Frankish conqueror, or a Christian reformer?
2. What can political authority do for virtue? What can it never do?
3. Why does literacy matter for religious and political order?
4. How should we judge historical figures whose virtues and vices are both large-scale?
5. Was the Carolingian Renaissance primarily intellectual, moral, religious, or administrative?
6. What parts of Charlemagne's project should a modern educator admire? What parts must be rejected?

---

## Key Takeaway

Charlemagne teaches that civilization is formed through institutions: schools, books, laws, worship, teachers, and disciplined habits. But his failures teach that formation loses its soul when it becomes coercion. The ruler may command behavior; he cannot conquer conscience.
