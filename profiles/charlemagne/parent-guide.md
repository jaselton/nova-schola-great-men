# Charlemagne: Parent Teaching Guide

---

## Overview: What This Profile Teaches

**Core Lesson:**  
Power is meant to serve formation: building schools, preserving books, strengthening worship, correcting injustice, and helping people become wiser and better.

**Secondary Lessons:**
- Leaders need teachers and wise counselors.
- Learning remains noble even for adults and rulers.
- Civilization depends on small disciplines: clear writing, accurate copying, honest judging, regular worship, and trained teachers.
- Good ends do not justify coercive or brutal means.

---

## Age-Appropriate Emphases

### Ages 8-10: The King Who Wanted Schools
**Focus on:**
- Charlemagne as a strong king who wanted people to learn
- The emperor practicing letters and honoring teachers
- Clear handwriting and careful copying
- Strength used to help others

**Avoid:**
- Graphic details of warfare
- Complex church-state questions
- Treating empire as automatically good

**Key Activities:**
- Clear copying challenge
- Design a kingdom school
- Make one good rule for the home or classroom
- Memory work on learning languages

---

### Ages 11-14: Power and Reform
**Focus on:**
- The difference between conquest and reform
- Why schools and books matter for civilization
- Alcuin as counselor and teacher
- The Saxon wars as a warning about coercion

**Complicate:**
- Charlemagne was great, but not simply "good" in every action.
- His reforms were religious and moral, not merely academic.
- Political unity and true conversion are not the same thing.

**Key Activities:**
- Conquest vs. reform chart
- Map Charlemagne's empire
- Design a reform program
- Moral judgment paragraph

---

### Ages 15-18: Empire and Moral Limits
**Focus on:**
- Carolingian Renaissance as formation at scale
- The *Admonitio Generalis* as a public education document
- The relationship between pope and emperor
- Ethical evaluation of Saxony and coerced conversion

**Philosophical Questions:**
- Can political power form virtue?
- What can law do, and what can only persuasion and grace do?
- How do we judge mixed historical greatness?
- Does cultural reform outlast political empire?

**Key Activities:**
- Source analysis of Einhard
- Debate on Charlemagne's legacy
- Comparative essay with Alexander, Augustine, Marcus Aurelius, or Napoleon
- Study of Carolingian minuscule and manuscript preservation

## Common Misconceptions to Address

### Misconception 1: "Charlemagne was just a conqueror"
**Reality:** He was a conqueror, but his lasting influence came through education, manuscripts, law, worship, and administrative reform.

**How to correct:** Ask: "If his empire divided, why do we still care about him?" Lead students to the preservation of books and reform of learning.

---

### Misconception 2: "If the goal is good, force is acceptable"
**Reality:** Charlemagne's Saxon policy shows the danger of coercive zeal. Christianity cannot be forced into the heart.

**How to correct:** Distinguish outward obedience from inward conviction. Ask: "Can fear make someone truly love what is good?"

---

### Misconception 3: "Education means modern school"
**Reality:** Carolingian education was monastic, clerical, liturgical, grammatical, and moral. Its aim was not career preparation but faithful worship and wise order.

**How to correct:** Compare modern schooling goals with Charlemagne's goals: literacy, prayer, accurate texts, moral discipline, public order.

---

### Misconception 4: "Great rulers do not need teachers"
**Reality:** Charlemagne needed Alcuin and other scholars. His greatness included the humility to honor learning he did not fully possess.

**How to correct:** Emphasize the writing tablets under his pillow. The emperor remained a student.

## Discussion Facilitation Tips

### For Younger Students (8-10)
Use concrete images:
- A king practicing letters
- A monk copying a book by candlelight
- A school beginning in a monastery
- A ruler making rules to help people learn

Ask:
- "What should strong people use their strength for?"
- "Why should a king care about children learning?"
- "What is one thing you still need to practice?"

---

### For Middle Students (11-14)
Introduce tradeoffs:
- "Conquest can unite land quickly; education forms people slowly. Which is more powerful?"
- "When does discipline become coercion?"
- "Why did Charlemagne need Alcuin?"

Require evidence:
- Have students name one reform and one failure.
- Do not allow either hero-worship or cynical dismissal.

---

### For Older Students (15-18)
Push ethical precision:
- "What acts of Charlemagne can be defended as just rule? Which cannot?"
- "What is the difference between enforcing public order and compelling conscience?"
- "How does the *Admonitio Generalis* reveal a theory of education?"

Encourage comparison:
- Alexander conquered and spread Hellenism.
- Augustine theorized Christian order.
- Alfred translated and educated under pressure.
- Napoleon reorganized Europe through secular law and administration.

## Sensitive Topics & How to Handle Them

### The Saxon Wars
**Why it matters:** This is the major moral warning in Charlemagne's life.

**How to present it:**
- Ages 8-10: "Charlemagne sometimes tried to force people to become Christian, and that was wrong. You cannot force the heart."
- Ages 11-14: Explain decades of rebellion and harsh punishment without graphic detail.
- Ages 15-18: Discuss Verden, forced baptism, Alcuin's warnings, and the ethics of coercion.

**Do not say:** "That was just how things were then," as if context removes moral judgment.

**Better:** "The context helps us understand why he acted as he did; it does not automatically justify what he did."

---

### Empire
**Risk:** Students may romanticize domination and scale.

**How to handle:**
- Ask what empire can do well: roads, law, protection, standardization, patronage.
- Ask what empire can do badly: crush local freedom, reward ambition, confuse unity with goodness.
- Emphasize formation over mere expansion.

---

### Christian Kingship
**Risk:** Modern students may either dismiss it as primitive or idealize it uncritically.

**How to handle:**
- Explain that Charlemagne believed rulers were responsible before God for the moral order of their people.
- Then ask what limits should govern that responsibility.
- Distinguish teaching, protecting, and correcting from coercing conscience.

## Extension Activities

### Manuscript Day
Have students copy a paragraph by hand as carefully as possible. Then introduce one intentional error and have another student find it. Discuss why copying accuracy mattered.

### Court of Charlemagne Roleplay
Assign roles:
- Charlemagne
- Alcuin
- A bishop
- A count
- A Saxon leader
- A monk-scribe
- A village parent

Question before the court: "How should the empire build schools and teach the faith without injustice?"

### Map and Movement
Trace Charlemagne's campaigns and court movements. Discuss how hard it was to govern without phones, trains, printing presses, or digital records.

### Compare Scripts
Show examples of Roman capitals, Merovingian scripts, and Carolingian minuscule. Ask which is easiest to read and why legibility changes civilization.

---

## Recommended Primary Source Passages

- Einhard on Charlemagne's studies and writing practice
- *Admonitio Generalis* sections on schools and corrected books
- Alcuin's letters on teaching faith and avoiding coercion
- Royal Frankish Annals on the coronation and Saxon wars, used carefully

---

## Parent Reflection

Charlemagne is especially useful because he resists simple treatment. He is neither a plaster saint nor a villain. He was a ruler of enormous energy who saw that a people must be formed by schools, worship, law, and learning. He was also capable of severity that violated the very spiritual formation he sought.

Teach him as a great man under judgment. That is the point of formation: not to flatten greatness, but to learn how virtue and vice can coexist in one powerful life—and how students must choose what to imitate.
