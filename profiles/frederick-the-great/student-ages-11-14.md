# Frederick the Great: Student Lesson (Ages 11-14)
## Discipline, Power, and the Rise of Prussia

---

## The Essential Question

**How can discipline make a leader strong, and how can power make him dangerous?**

---

## A Prince Under Pressure

Frederick was born in 1712 into the Prussian royal family. His father, Frederick William I, built a disciplined, military state. He valued obedience, thrift, and soldiers. Frederick preferred music, books, French literature, and philosophy.

Their conflict became severe. In 1730 Frederick tried to escape. The plan failed, and his friend Hans Hermann von Katte was executed. Frederick was imprisoned and forced into state service. This experience helped form his self-control, but it also hardened him.

---

## The Working King

Frederick became king in 1740. He believed a ruler should work for the state. His famous idea was that the sovereign is the “first servant of the State.”

He was not a modern democratic leader. He was an absolute monarch. But he did not think kingship should be lazy. He inspected troops, reviewed officials, worked on law and administration, promoted religious toleration, supported economic development, wrote books, played music, and corresponded with Enlightenment thinkers.

His life shows that leadership requires attention to details most people never see.

---

## Silesia and the Moral Problem

Soon after becoming king, Frederick invaded Silesia, a wealthy province ruled by Austria's Habsburgs. His claim was weak, but his opportunity was strong. Maria Theresa had just inherited the Habsburg lands, and Prussia had a prepared army.

The conquest made Prussia stronger. It also helped begin the War of the Austrian Succession and set up later conflict in the Seven Years' War.

This is the central moral problem of Frederick's life:

> He was disciplined and brilliant, but he used those gifts for aggressive power.

A student must learn to ask two questions:

1. Did it work?
2. Was it right?

Those are not the same question.

---

## The Seven Years' War

From 1756 to 1763, Prussia fought for survival against powerful enemies including Austria, France, Russia, Saxony, and Sweden. Britain became Frederick's main ally.

Frederick won great victories, especially Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757. He also suffered terrible losses. Prussia survived through training, discipline, maneuver, money from Britain, enemy disunity, and fortune.

The war proved Frederick's endurance. It also showed the terrible cost of ambition and great-power politics.

---

## Culture and Command

Frederick was not only a soldier. He built Sanssouci, loved French culture, played the flute, wrote in French, and corresponded with Voltaire.

This matters because students sometimes divide life into false categories: strong people do not read, artistic people cannot command, thinkers cannot act. Frederick breaks that pattern.

But culture did not automatically make him gentle. Music and philosophy must be joined to moral restraint.

---

## Lessons to Keep

Frederick should be imitated for:

- hard work under responsibility
- discipline before crisis
- seriousness about law and administration
- courage under pressure
- love of learning and music

Frederick should be questioned for:

- invading weaker or vulnerable neighbors
- treating state interest as the highest good
- becoming cold and severe
- letting victory cover moral questions

---

## Practice: Two-Column Judgment

Make a chart with two columns.

**Column 1: What Frederick did well**  
Include discipline, work, reform, military courage, culture.

**Column 2: What must be judged carefully**  
Include Silesia, war, ambition, coldness, power.

Then write one paragraph:

> Frederick was great in some ways and dangerous in others because ___

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Why did Frederick and his father clash?
2. What does “first servant of the State” mean?
3. Was the conquest of Silesia wise, wrong, or both?
4. Why is discipline not automatically virtue?
5. Can a person love music and still be harsh?
6. What kind of power needs the most restraint?

---

## Closing Lesson

Frederick teaches that responsibility requires disciplined work. But he also teaches that a disciplined person can still choose wrongly. Strength must be governed by justice, or it becomes dangerous.
