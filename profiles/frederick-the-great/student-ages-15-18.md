# Frederick the Great: Student Lesson (Ages 15-18)
## Enlightened Absolutism and the Moral Danger of Success

---

## The Essential Question

**Can a ruler be both an enlightened servant of the state and an aggressive conqueror?**

---

## Frederick's Contradiction

Frederick II of Prussia is one of the clearest examples of enlightened absolutism. He valued reason, administration, legal reform, religious toleration, economic improvement, music, literature, and correspondence with philosophes. He also ruled as an absolute monarch and made Prussia a military great power through war.

This contradiction is not a footnote. It is the center of the lesson.

Frederick's own phrase, “the sovereign is the first servant of the State,” expresses a serious vision of rule. The monarch is not merely owner of the realm. He is obligated to labor for its welfare.

But if the state becomes the highest good, service can become dangerous. A ruler may sacrifice neighbors, soldiers, and subjects to the glory or security of the state.

---

## Formation: Severity and Self-Command

Frederick's father, Frederick William I, formed Prussia into a disciplined military and administrative state. He also treated his son harshly. Frederick loved music, French literature, and intellectual society; his father wanted obedience, austerity, and military seriousness.

The failed escape of 1730 and the execution of Katte in Frederick's presence were formative traumas. They taught Frederick the terror of authority and the necessity of self-command. But they may also have helped produce emotional coldness.

Formation does not excuse later action, but it helps explain the man: cultivated, controlled, brilliant, and severe.

---

## Anti-Machiavel and Political Reality

Before or around his accession, Frederick wrote *Anti-Machiavel*, a critique of Machiavelli's *Prince*, edited and expanded through Voltaire's involvement. In it, Frederick argued against princely deception and understood that rulers could not hide their real character forever.

Then, in 1740, he invaded Silesia.

The invasion was strategically intelligent. Austria was vulnerable after Charles VI's death, Maria Theresa's succession was contested, and Silesia was rich and important. But the moral claim was weak. Frederick's action helped begin the War of the Austrian Succession.

This is why Frederick is educationally powerful. He forces students to judge the gap between political theory and political action.

---

## War, Survival, and Greatness

The Seven Years' War nearly destroyed Prussia. Austria sought to recover Silesia; France, Russia, Saxony, and Sweden aligned against Prussia, while Britain supported Frederick. Frederick's victories at Rossbach and Leuthen in 1757 became symbols of tactical brilliance. Yet he also suffered devastating losses and depended on allied support, enemy mistakes, and geopolitical fortune.

By the Treaty of Hubertusburg in 1763, Prussia retained Silesia. Frederick had survived. Prussia's great-power status was secured.

But students must ask: does survival vindicate the earlier seizure? Does endurance in a war erase responsibility for helping create the conditions of war?

---

## Reform and State Service

Frederick's domestic rule was not empty propaganda. He pursued administrative and judicial reforms, promoted professional standards in bureaucracy and courts, supported economic development, and encouraged religious toleration. German History in Documents and Images notes his abolition of judicial torture and reforms that opened royal appeals courts to villagers in conflict with noble lordships.

He worked constantly. This is the best meaning of “first servant.” He accepted the burden of governing.

Yet enlightened absolutism remained absolutism. Reform flowed from the king's will. Subjects were served, but they did not rule themselves.

---

## The Ethical Judgment

Frederick should be admired for:

- personal industry in office
- disciplined administration
- courage under pressure
- intellectual cultivation
- legal and bureaucratic seriousness
- refusal to treat kingship as mere luxury

Frederick should be morally criticized for:

- aggressive war
- opportunism
- coldness toward suffering
- treating state interest as an overriding principle
- inconsistency between philosophical ideals and political action

The mature judgment is not simple admiration or simple denunciation. It is moral discrimination.

---

## Primary Source Practice

Read Frederick's line from the *Political Testament*:

> “The sovereign is the first servant of the State.”

Write one page answering:

1. What does this claim get right about leadership?
2. What danger appears if the state becomes the highest good?
3. How does Frederick's conquest of Silesia test the sincerity or limits of the claim?

---

## Comparative Seminar

Compare Frederick with George Washington.

Questions:

- How did each man understand public duty?
- What did each do with military success?
- Which man placed clearer limits on personal power?
- Can Frederick's service ethic be separated from absolutism?

---

## Essay Prompt

Write 1,000-1,400 words:

> Frederick the Great shows that disciplined leadership is not enough; power must be governed by justice.

Include:
- the Katte crisis or early formation
- Silesia
- the Seven Years' War
- “first servant of the State”
- one domestic reform
- a comparison to another figure in the Great Men sequence

---

## Discussion Questions

1. Is enlightened absolutism a contradiction in terms?
2. How should we judge Frederick's invasion of Silesia?
3. What is the difference between serving the state and serving justice?
4. Did Frederick's culture make him more humane?
5. Can a ruler be great if his greatness depends on war?
6. Why is Washington a useful contrast to Frederick?

---

## Closing Lesson

Frederick's life warns against shallow definitions of greatness. He was not lazy, foolish, or merely cruel. He was disciplined, intelligent, cultivated, and serious. But those qualities served ambition as well as reform.

The student should leave Frederick with a sharpened moral question:

> What good is strength if justice does not command it?
