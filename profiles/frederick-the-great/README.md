# Frederick the Great
## The King Who Made Himself the First Servant of the State

**Born:** January 24, 1712, Berlin, Prussia  
**Died:** August 17, 1786, Potsdam, near Berlin  
**Era:** Modern / Enlightenment Europe  
**Domain:** Kingship, Military Command, Statecraft, Law, Culture

---

## The One-Minute Story

Frederick the Great was a prince who loved music, books, French literature, and philosophy, but he was raised by a harsh father who demanded military obedience. As a young man, Frederick tried to flee. The plan failed, his friend Katte was executed, and Frederick learned how terrible power could be.

When he became king of Prussia in 1740, he did not become lazy. He worked. He read reports, inspected troops, supervised officials, supported legal reform, corresponded with philosophers, played the flute, and built Sanssouci. He described the sovereign as “the first servant of the State.”

But Frederick also seized Silesia from Austria and helped plunge Europe into war. He made Prussia powerful, but at a grave human cost. He teaches students discipline, duty, strategic clarity, and the seriousness of leadership.

He also warns them: power can make a brilliant person cold. Success is not the same as justice.

---

## Why He Matters for Your Child

**Frederick teaches:**
- That leadership is work before privilege
- How discipline and preparation can preserve a people in crisis
- Why culture and command can belong together
- The importance of law, administration, and attention to detail
- How serious responsibility demands personal industry

**But also warns against:**
- Ambition without moral restraint
- Treating victory as proof of justice
- Hardness of soul
- Using the state as an excuse for aggression
- Saying noble things while acting opportunistically

## Formation Signature

### Virtues (What to Imitate)
- **Industry** — working steadily under the burden of responsibility
- **Discipline** — preparing before crisis comes
- **Strategic clarity** — seeing the real problem and acting decisively
- **Administrative seriousness** — caring about law, officials, courts, taxes, and details
- **Cultivation** — keeping music, reading, and philosophy alive amid public duty

### Risks (What to Guard Against)
- **Aggression** — confusing opportunity with right
- **Coldness** — letting discipline become contempt
- **Cynicism** — separating public words from actual conduct
- **State idolatry** — treating state power as the highest good
- **Pride of success** — assuming victory justifies itself

### How He Was Formed
- **Severe Prussian court** → obedience, military discipline, fear of failure
- **Conflict with Frederick William I** → inward resistance, secrecy, self-command
- **Katte crisis** → terrible lesson in power and consequence
- **Rheinsberg years** → books, music, French Enlightenment culture, political writing
- **Prussian army and bureaucracy** → inherited instruments of discipline and state power
- **Wars for Silesia** → ambition tested by danger, endurance, and cost

## Quick Practice Ideas

**Ages 8-10:**
- Make a “leader's work list” for a king, parent, teacher, or team captain
- Practice: do one useful job before taking a privilege
- Memorize: “A leader serves before he is served.”

**Ages 11-14:**
- Map Prussia, Austria, and Silesia
- Compare Frederick the musician and Frederick the soldier
- Discuss: Can winning ever hide wrongdoing?

**Ages 15-18:**
- Analyze the line: “The sovereign is the first servant of the State”
- Debate: Is enlightened absolutism a contradiction?
- Essay: “Was Frederick great, dangerous, or both?”
