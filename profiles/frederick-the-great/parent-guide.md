# Parent Guide: How to Teach Frederick the Great

---

## Why Frederick Belongs in the Curriculum

Frederick the Great is essential for teaching leadership, discipline, statecraft, and moral judgment about power.

He is not a simple hero. That is why he is useful. He worked hard, cultivated the arts, reformed parts of government, and treated kingship as duty. He also invaded Silesia, fought aggressive wars, and could be cold, cynical, and severe.

Teach him as a disciplined ruler whose virtues were real, and whose flaws were dangerous.

---

## Core Formation Aim

By the end of this profile, your student should understand:

> Leadership means service and work, but power must be ruled by justice.

Frederick helps students resist two errors: worshiping successful power and dismissing serious achievement because it is morally mixed.

---

## What to Emphasize by Age

### Ages 8-10

Focus on:
- leaders should serve first
- kingship as work, not luxury
- Frederick's love of music and books
- the difference between being strong and being good
- doing a duty before taking a privilege

Avoid detailed military politics. Keep the moral frame simple.

**Main sentence:**  
“A leader serves before he is served.”

### Ages 11-14

Add:
- conflict with his father
- the failed escape and Katte crisis
- Silesia as a morally complicated conquest
- discipline and preparation
- the Seven Years' War as survival under pressure

This age can handle the distinction between strategic success and moral right.

**Main sentence:**  
“Winning does not prove justice.”

### Ages 15-18

Add:
- enlightened absolutism
- *Anti-Machiavel* and the gap between theory and action
- the Political Testament's “first servant” line
- War of the Austrian Succession and Seven Years' War
- legal and administrative reform
- comparison with Washington, Napoleon, Marcus Aurelius, or Charlemagne

Older students should practice moral discrimination: admire what is admirable, condemn what is unjust.

**Main sentence:**  
“Power becomes dangerous when the state replaces justice.”

## Virtues to Draw Out

### Industry

Frederick worked constantly. He did not treat authority as leisure.

Ask: **What duty comes with the authority you want?**

### Discipline

Prussia's strength depended on preparation before crisis.

Ask: **What habits are you building before you need them?**

### Strategic Clarity

Frederick could identify real pressures and act decisively.

Ask: **What is the actual problem, not merely the loudest problem?**

### Cultivation

Frederick kept music, reading, writing, and conversation in his life.

Ask: **How can strength and learning support each other?**

---

## Risks to Name Clearly

### Aggression

Frederick's seizure of Silesia cannot be treated as morally neutral just because it succeeded.

Ask: **Are you doing this because it is right, or because you can?**

### Coldness

Discipline can become contempt when separated from charity.

Ask: **Are high standards making you better, or merely harsher?**

### State Idolatry

“The first servant of the state” is noble in one sense, but dangerous if the state becomes the highest good.

Ask: **What is higher than success, order, or power?**

### Inconsistency

Frederick criticized Machiavellian deception, yet often acted opportunistically.

Ask: **Do your actions match your stated principles?**

---

## Common Teaching Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Making Frederick a Cartoon Militarist

He was a soldier-king, but also a musician, writer, reader, builder, and reforming administrator.

### Mistake 2: Making Him a Pure Enlightenment Hero

His reforms and toleration matter, but so do Silesia, war, and absolutism.

### Mistake 3: Treating “Great” as “Good”

Greatness in historical consequence is not the same as moral goodness.

### Mistake 4: Ignoring the Human Cost of War

Students should understand that maps and victories represent real suffering.

### Mistake 5: Equating Service to the State with Service to Justice

The state can be served well while justice is violated. This is one of Frederick's central warnings.

---

## Discussion Prompts for the Family

1. What should a leader do before enjoying privileges?
2. Why might a cultivated person still act harshly?
3. Was taking Silesia a sign of greatness, wrongdoing, or both?
4. What is the difference between discipline and virtue?
5. What should be higher than the success of the state?
6. How is Frederick different from Washington?

---

## Simple Assessment

Ask your student to complete these sentences:

- Frederick believed a ruler should be ___.
- Frederick's love of music shows ___.
- Silesia matters because ___.
- Winning does not prove ___.
- Frederick's life warns me that ___.
- I can practice servant leadership by ___.

---

## Parent Takeaway

Frederick is best taught as a test case in morally mixed greatness.

He gives students a strong lesson in duty: leadership is work. He gives them an equally strong warning: disciplined power without justice can become dangerous.
