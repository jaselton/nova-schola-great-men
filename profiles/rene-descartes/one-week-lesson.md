# René Descartes: One-Week Lesson Plan

---

## Overview

**Target:** All ages (select activities appropriate to your student's level)  
**Time commitment:** 45-60 minutes/day  
**Structure:** 5 days, Monday-Friday  
**Goal:** Introduce Descartes's life, method, and lessons through story, problem-solving, discussion, and writing

---

## Before You Begin

### Materials Needed
- Notebook or paper
- Pencil or pen
- Index cards or sticky notes
- Ruler or graph paper for older students
- Optional: short excerpt from *Discourse on Method* or *Meditations on First Philosophy*

### Choose Your Age Band
- **Ages 8-10:** Focus on clear thinking, breaking problems into steps, and simple discussion
- **Ages 11-14:** Add methodic doubt, the cogito, and debate over helpful versus harmful doubt
- **Ages 15-18:** Add primary source reading, mind-body dualism, and analytical writing

---

## Monday: The Story

### Morning: Read Together (20-30 min)

**Ages 8-10:** Read the [student lesson ages 8-10](./student-ages-8-10.md) together  
**Ages 11-14:** Read the [student lesson ages 11-14](./student-ages-11-14.md) — or have them read independently  
**Ages 15-18:** Assign the [student lesson ages 15-18](./student-ages-15-18.md) as independent reading

**As you read, pause to discuss:**
- “Why did Descartes care so much about certainty?”
- “What does it mean to think in order?”
- “Can doubt be helpful without becoming destructive?”

---

### Afternoon: Start the Method Notebook (15-20 min)

**Activity:** Set up Descartes's method

**What to do:**
1. Give your student a notebook or section of a notebook
2. Title the first page: “My Method Notebook”
3. Copy this sentence:

> It is not enough to have a good mind; the main thing is to use it well.

4. On the next page, write Descartes's student method:

- Ask clearly
- Divide the problem
- Work in order
- Check carefully

**Reflection question:**

> What is one area where I need clearer thinking this week?

---

## Tuesday: The Four-Step Method

### Morning: Memory Work (5 min)

**All ages:** Memorize the simplified method:

> Ask clearly. Divide the problem. Work in order. Check carefully.

**How to practice:**
- Say it together 5 times
- Student says it alone 3 times
- Ask: “Which step do you usually skip?”

---

### Afternoon: Method Practice (30-40 min)

**Activity:** Apply Descartes's method to a real problem

**What to do:**

Choose one problem:
- a math problem
- planning an essay
- cleaning a messy room
- preparing for a test
- deciding how to spend an afternoon well

Make four boxes:

1. **Clear Question:** What exactly am I trying to solve?
2. **Parts:** What smaller pieces does this problem have?
3. **Order:** What should I do first, second, third?
4. **Review:** How will I know I did not miss anything?

**Younger students:** Parent may help fill in the boxes  
**Older students:** Student should complete independently, then explain the plan

**Discussion questions:**
- “What changed when the problem was divided?”
- “Which part became easier?”
- “Did the method slow you down or help you move faster?”

---

## Wednesday: Doubt and Certainty

### Morning: Socratic Discussion (20-30 min)

**Pick 3-5 questions appropriate to your student's age:**

**Ages 8-10:**
1. What does it mean to be sure about something?
2. Can your eyes ever trick you?
3. Why should you ask questions before answering?
4. When is it time to stop questioning and act?

**Ages 11-14:**
1. What is the difference between doubt and cynicism?
2. Why did Descartes doubt the senses?
3. What does “I think, therefore I am” prove?
4. Can a person doubt too much?

**Ages 15-18:**
1. Is methodic doubt a good foundation for philosophy?
2. Does the cogito prove only existence, or more than existence?
3. Can knowledge begin from the isolated thinking self?
4. What might Descartes miss by seeking certainty first?

**How to facilitate:**
- Ask the question
- Wait before answering
- Require reasons, not slogans
- Ask: “What would Descartes say?” and “What might his critic say?”

---

### Afternoon: Copywork and Explanation (15-20 min)

**Ages 8-10:**

> I think, therefore I am.

Copy 3 times. Then write: “This means ___.”

**Ages 11-14:**

> It is not enough to have a good mind; the main thing is to use it well.

Copy once. Then write 4-5 sentences about what “using the mind well” means for a student.

**Ages 15-18:**

> Some years ago I was struck by the large number of falsehoods that I had accepted as true in my childhood.

Copy once from the opening theme of the *Meditations*. Then write 5-7 sentences: What kind of intellectual courage is Descartes practicing here? What danger accompanies it?

---

## Thursday: Hands-On Project

### All Day: Divide and Translate (45-75 min total, can be split)

**Activity:** Descartes's mathematical habit — translate a problem into a clearer form

Choose a project appropriate to age/skill:

**Ages 8-10: Chore Map**
- Pick a messy space
- Draw it as a simple map
- Divide it into zones
- Clean one zone at a time
- Check the map when finished

**Ages 11-14: Essay Builder**
- Choose a short essay question
- Turn it into an outline
- Divide into introduction, three reasons, conclusion
- Write one paragraph
- Check whether every sentence helps the main claim

**Ages 15-18: Coordinate Thinking**
- On graph paper, plot simple points and connect them
- Write a simple equation or pattern if appropriate
- Discuss how geometry can become algebra
- Then choose a non-math problem and ask: “What would be the equivalent of graphing this?”

**Process:**
1. Name the problem
2. Translate it into a diagram, outline, table, or graph
3. Solve one part
4. Review the result
5. Explain how translation clarified the problem

---

## Friday: Synthesis and Virtue

### Morning: Review (20-30 min)

Ask the student to retell Descartes's life in five points:

1. French childhood and strong education
2. Dissatisfaction with uncertain philosophy
3. Search for method
4. “I think, therefore I am”
5. Influence and warning

Then ask:

> What should we imitate? What should we be careful not to imitate?

---

### Afternoon: Final Assignment (30-45 min)

**Ages 8-10:**
Draw Descartes with a staircase of clear thinking. Label each stair: question, parts, order, check.

Write one sentence:

> I can use my mind well by ___.

**Ages 11-14:**
Write 1-2 paragraphs:

> Doubt is helpful when ___. Doubt is dangerous when ___.

Include one example from Descartes and one example from your own life.

**Ages 15-18:**
Write 700-1,000 words:

> Descartes's method is still useful, but his philosophy must be corrected by a fuller view of the human person.

Use at least two of these: method, cogito, analytic geometry, mind-body dualism, Princess Elisabeth.

---

## Optional Extension Activities

### For All Ages
- Make a “clear thinking checklist” for schoolwork
- Use Descartes's four steps on one family decision
- Draw a comic strip of Descartes trying to doubt everything

### For Ages 11-14
- Compare Descartes and Galileo: observation versus method
- Research what Cartesian coordinates are
- Create a poster: “Doubt is a tool, not a home”

### For Ages 15-18
- Read selections from *Meditations I-II*
- Debate the Cartesian Circle
- Compare Descartes's dualism with Aristotle's hylomorphism or a modern neuroscience account

---

## Parent Debrief

At the end of the week, ask:

1. Did the student learn the four-step method?
2. Can the student explain the cogito without turning it into a slogan?
3. Does the student understand the difference between useful doubt and corrosive doubt?
4. Did the student apply method to a real problem?
5. Can the student name both Descartes's greatness and his risk?

**Final sentence to remember:**

> Think clearly, divide wisely, doubt carefully, and seek truth.
