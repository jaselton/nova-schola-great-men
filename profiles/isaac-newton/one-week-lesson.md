# Isaac Newton: One-Week Lesson Plan

---

## Overview

**Target:** All ages (select activities appropriate to your student's level)  
**Time commitment:** 45-60 minutes/day  
**Structure:** 5 days, Monday-Friday  
**Goal:** Introduce Newton's life, method, and lessons through story, observation, experiment, and discussion

---

## Before You Begin

### Materials Needed
- Notebook or science journal
- Pencil
- Stopwatch or phone timer
- String and a small safe weight for pendulum activity
- Ball or other safe falling objects
- Optional: prism, glass of water, flashlight, or sunlight for light activity
- Optional: images of Newton, Woolsthorpe, the reflecting telescope, and the *Principia*

### Choose Your Age Band
- **Ages 8-10:** Focus on story, gravity, simple observation, and kindness under correction
- **Ages 11-14:** Add pendulum timing, prism/light observation, and discussion of solitude
- **Ages 15-18:** Add primary source work, Newton vs. Descartes, and methodological analysis

---

## Monday: The Story

### Morning: Read Together (20-30 min)

**Ages 8-10:** Read the [student lesson ages 8-10](./student-ages-8-10.md) together  
**Ages 11-14:** Read the [student lesson ages 11-14](./student-ages-11-14.md) — or have them read independently  
**Ages 15-18:** Assign the [student lesson ages 15-18](./student-ages-15-18.md) as independent reading

**As you read, pause to discuss:**
- “How did Newton use quiet time?”
- “Why is the apple story not the whole point?”
- “What was Newton good at? What was he bad at?”

---

### Afternoon: Start a Newton Notebook (15-20 min)

**Activity:** Set up a journal of observation and measurement

**What to do:**
1. Write on the first page: “[Student's name]'s Newton Notebook — Observation, Number, and Law”
2. Add the date
3. Copy this sentence:

> Look carefully before you guess.

**First entry:**
Observe one ordinary motion: a falling object, a rolling ball, a swinging door, or water pouring.

Write or draw:
- What moved?
- What direction did it move?
- Did it speed up, slow down, or stay steady?
- What question does it raise?

---

## Tuesday: Gravity and Motion

### Morning: Memory Work (5 min)

**All ages:** Memorize:

> Look carefully before you guess.

**Older students may also memorize:**

> “I do not feign hypotheses.”  
> — Isaac Newton

---

### Afternoon: Falling Objects Observation (30-40 min)

**Activity:** Observe gravity without rushing to explain

**What to do:**
1. Choose three safe objects: ball, pencil, sock, paper, eraser, etc.
2. Drop each from the same height.
3. Record what happens.
4. Try a flat sheet of paper, then crumple the paper and drop it again.

**Questions:**
- What fell fastest?
- Did shape matter?
- What did you observe vs. what did you assume?
- What would you need to test more carefully?

**Ages 15-18 extension:**
Distinguish gravity from air resistance. Write a paragraph on why ordinary observation can be misleading unless carefully controlled.

---

## Wednesday: Pendulum and Pattern

### Morning: Build a Simple Pendulum (20-30 min)

**Activity:** Measure a swinging object

**What to do:**
1. Tie a small safe weight to string.
2. Swing it gently.
3. Time 10 swings.
4. Shorten the string and time 10 swings again.
5. Lengthen the string and repeat.

**Record:**
- string length
- time for 10 swings
- what changed
- what stayed similar

---

### Afternoon: Socratic Discussion (20-30 min)

Pick 3-5 questions appropriate to age.

**Ages 8-10:**
1. Why did Newton ask questions about falling things?
2. What did you notice in the pendulum?
3. Why should we look before guessing?
4. How can quiet time help you learn?

**Ages 11-14:**
1. What is the difference between watching and measuring?
2. Why did Newton need mathematics?
3. Was solitude good for Newton?
4. How should you respond when someone criticizes your work?

**Ages 15-18:**
1. What does it mean to derive forces from phenomena?
2. Why is “I do not feign hypotheses” important?
3. How did Newton's method differ from Descartes's?
4. Can intellectual greatness exist without moral maturity?

---

## Thursday: Light and Experiment

### Morning: Prism or Rainbow Observation (30-45 min)

**Activity:** Study light like Newton

**Options:**
- Use a prism in sunlight
- Shine a flashlight through a prism
- Use a glass of water near sunlight to find color
- Look at rainbow effects in soap bubbles or oil on water

**Record:**
- What colors appeared?
- What changed when the angle changed?
- What question would Newton ask?

**Older student extension:**
Research Newton's prism experiments and explain why they challenged the idea that color is merely a modification of white light.

---

### Afternoon: Copywork (15 min)

**Ages 8-10:**
> Look carefully before you guess.

Copy 3 times neatly.

**Ages 11-14:**
> “If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of Giants.”  
> — Isaac Newton

Copy once. Then write: Who were Newton's “giants”?

**Ages 15-18:**
> “I have not as yet been able to deduce from phenomena the reason for these properties of gravity, and I do not feign hypotheses.”  
> — Isaac Newton

Copy once. Then write 3-4 sentences explaining what Newton refuses to claim.

---

## Friday: Synthesis

### Morning: Character Discussion (20-30 min)

Discuss Newton's virtues and flaws.

**Virtues:** concentration, exactness, patience, courage, restraint  
**Flaws:** suspicion, harshness, secrecy, resentment, overcontrol

Ask:
- Which virtue do you most need?
- Which flaw should you guard against?
- How can a smart person stay humble?

---

### Afternoon: Final Project (45-60 min)

Choose one:

**Ages 8-10: Gravity Poster**
- Draw an apple, Earth, Moon, and arrows for gravity
- Add one sentence: “Newton taught me ___.”

**Ages 11-14: Experiment Report**
- Write a one-page report on either falling objects, pendulum, or light
- Include: question, method, observation, conclusion, next test

**Ages 15-18: Analytical Essay**
- Write 900-1,200 words:

> Newton's greatness came from disciplined restraint as much as from imagination.

Include the *Principia*, optics, and one character flaw.

---

## Optional Weekend Extensions

- Watch a documentary segment on Newton and the *Principia*
- Visit a science museum or planetarium
- Research Edmond Halley's role in getting the *Principia* published
- Compare Newton and Galileo in a short chart
- Compare Newton and Descartes on method

---

## Parent Closing Script

“Newton teaches us that the world is ordered, but we must not be lazy in looking for that order. We observe, measure, think, and test. We also remember that being brilliant is not enough. A great mind should be joined to humility, patience, and kindness.”
